The tabs are open because closing them feels like losing them. That's the actual problem — not the memory usage, not the clutter. Somewhere in those 47 tabs is a comparison you half-finished, a video you meant to watch, and an article you'll definitely need next week, and there's no way you're hunting them all down again.

Marqly resolves it with one click: the tab saver captures every open tab into your library, AI tags each page automatically, and semantic search finds any of them later by description. Then you close the window. All of it, guilt-free.

## One click, every tab

Marqly's tab saver takes the whole window at once. You don't triage tabs one at a time, deciding which ones are worth keeping — the honest answer is you don't know yet, which is why they're all still open. Saving everything in a single click removes the decision entirely.

This matters most at the exact moments tab debt piles up: the end of a research session, the laptop-is-crawling moment, the "I need this machine for a presentation in five minutes" moment. One click, and the entire window's worth of open work is stored. If you want the mechanics spelled out, see [how do I save all my open tabs](/faq/how-do-i-save-all-my-open-tabs).

## No filing session afterward

The reason most save-my-tabs tools don't stick is what happens after the save: a dump of 47 unlabeled links you're supposed to sort into folders. Nobody does that. The dump becomes a junk drawer, the junk drawer becomes ignorable, and you're back to hoarding tabs.

Marqly skips the filing session because AI does it on save. Every page gets tagged automatically — the article about email deliverability gets tagged as such without you touching anything. There's no inbox-zero ritual, no weekly cleanup you'll abandon by March. Why tag-based, automatic organization beats hand-built folder trees is a longer argument, made in [how to organize bookmarks](/blog/how-to-organize-bookmarks).

AI summaries handle the other half of the post-save problem: a dumped window usually contains a handful of long articles you saved on faith. Each one gets a summary, so triaging what's actually worth reading takes a scan, not an afternoon of reopening tabs to remember why you kept them.

One more thing worth knowing if tab hoarding isn't your only backlog: Marqly imports Pocket exports, Raindrop.io collections, and standard browser bookmark HTML files. The five hundred bookmarks you've been dragging between browsers since 2019 can come in through the same door and get the same automatic tagging.

## Closing is safe when finding is easy

Here's the test of any tab manager: two weeks after you saved and closed everything, can you actually get a specific page back? With folders, that depends on whether Past You filed it where Present You is looking. With Marqly, you just describe it.

Semantic search works by meaning, not keywords — "the pricing teardown with the spreadsheet screenshots" finds the right save even though none of those words are in the title. It searches across titles, page content, highlights, and transcripts. The practical effect: closing a tab stops being a risk, because retrieval no longer depends on remembering exact titles or filing correctly. There's a fuller walkthrough of this in [how to search bookmarks with AI](/blog/how-to-search-bookmarks-with-ai).

And you don't have to leave your current page to check what you've saved: the side-panel library opens alongside whatever you're reading, so "did I already save this?" takes two seconds to answer.

## Who this is not for

Marqly is not a workspace or session manager. If your job is switching between project workspaces — restore these 12 tabs for Client A at 9am, swap to Client B's window layout at 2pm — that's Workona's territory, and Marqly doesn't compete there. It has no concept of reopening a saved window arrangement.

It's also not a tab suspender: it won't keep tabs open-but-frozen to save memory, because its answer to tab overload is that most of those tabs shouldn't be open at all — they should be saved, closed, and retrievable.

The fit is this: your open tabs are mostly things you're afraid to lose, not things you're actively using. If that describes your window right now, a one-click save into a library that searches by meaning is the fix — not another tool for arranging the hoard.
