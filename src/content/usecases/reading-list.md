Every reading list app has the same failure mode: saving takes one tap, but nothing ever comes back out. The list grows, the guilt grows with it, and eventually you declare bankruptcy and start a new one. The problem was never the saving — it's that a list of 400 titles gives you no way to decide what's worth your next 20 minutes, and no way to find the one piece you actually need later. Marqly attacks both ends: an AI summary on every save so you can triage the backlog fast, and semantic search that finds any save from whatever fragment you remember about it.

## Articles and videos in the same list

Half of what you "mean to get back to" isn't an article anymore — it's a 40-minute YouTube video. Article-only read-later apps make you keep that second list somewhere else, usually YouTube's own Watch Later, where it goes to die separately.

Marqly treats both as saves in one library. Articles come in through the extension's one-click save; videos come in through a bookmark button Marqly adds directly to YouTube's action row, which saves the video with its transcript attached. That transcript isn't decoration — it's what makes the video findable later by what was said in it, and it feeds the YouTube AI card's summary tab, so you can get the TL;DR and key sections of a long video before deciding whether it earns a full watch. On Pro, the card's chat tab answers questions straight from the transcript.

Ending a tab-hoarding session is one click too: the tab saver captures every open tab at once, so "I'll read these later" stops meaning "I'll keep 30 tabs open until my laptop fan complains."

## Triage the backlog with AI summaries

The difference between a reading list and a guilt pile is triage, and triage is exactly what raw bookmarks make impossible — a title and a URL tell you almost nothing three weeks later.

In Marqly, every saved article carries an AI summary. Working through a backlog becomes a quick pass: read the summary, then read in full, keep for reference, or archive without ceremony. Thirty seconds per item instead of five minutes of re-skimming, which is the difference between actually doing the pass and never starting it.

Auto-tagging handles the organizing you were never going to do by hand. Every save is tagged automatically, so themes emerge on their own — you don't build a folder taxonomy, and you don't pay the filing tax at save time that makes people abandon organized systems within a week.

## Recall beats scrolling

The deeper problem with reading lists is that scrolling is the only interface. If you can't remember the title, the save is effectively gone.

Marqly's semantic search works the way memory does: describe the thing. "That essay about attention and note-taking," "the video about sourdough starters" — the search matches meaning, not keywords, across titles, page content, your highlights, and video transcripts. Our walkthrough on [searching bookmarks with AI](/blog/how-to-search-bookmarks-with-ai) shows what this looks like against a real backlog.

For the pieces you do read, the highlighter makes the reading stick. Select any text on a page, highlight it in one of six colors, add a note. Highlights persist on the page when you revisit and sync to your library — and because search covers highlights too, the best sentence you read this year is retrievable by roughly remembering it. Pro users can also chat with their saves: ask a question and get an answer drawn from your own saved content, which turns a reading list into something closer to a personal reference desk.
