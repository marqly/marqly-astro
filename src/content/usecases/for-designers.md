Design inspiration has a shelf life measured in scroll-lengths. You pass a landing page with a navigation treatment worth stealing, a case study that explains a rebrand honestly, a portfolio whose typography does something you've never seen — and by the time a project actually needs that reference, it's a ghost. "It had a dark hero and the serifs were doing something weird" is all you've got, and browser bookmarks can't answer a query like that. Screenshots-in-camera-roll can't either.

Marqly is an AI bookmark manager that fits how designers actually collect: fast capture mid-browse, automatic organization, boards for projects, and search that works on descriptions like the one above.

## Boards that work like project moodboards

Boards group links and highlights around whatever axis you think in — one per client project, or standing collections like "pricing pages," "onboarding flows," "editorial typography." Saving is one click from the toolbar, so capture never interrupts browsing, and when a research binge ends with a dozen inspiring tabs open, the tab saver stores the entire window in one action.

Capture keeps pace with wherever browsing happens, too. The side-panel library sits alongside the page you're on, so mid-scroll you can check whether a reference already exists in a board without switching contexts. And since half of inspiration strikes on the couch, the iOS app catches what you find on your phone — while desktop extensions for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari cover every studio setup.

The share angle is genuinely useful for client work: any board becomes a public page viewable without a Marqly account. Instead of pasting fifteen URLs into a kickoff doc, you send one link to a curated, up-to-date reference collection. Stakeholders click through in their own browser; nobody installs anything.

## References that don't rot

Live URLs are a fragile medium for design references — sites get redesigned, and the redesign is usually why you saved them. Marqly's save-as-PDF captures any page as a clean PDF matching the on-screen layout, with lazy-loaded images included, processed locally in your browser. The version of the page that impressed you is preserved even after the site changes underneath the link.

If the pattern you want to keep is interaction-heavy — a scroll-triggered animation, a stepped signup flow — save the live URL and the PDF together on the same board: the PDF holds the layout, the link holds the behavior for as long as it lasts. Spec references age the same way — platform crop and safe-zone rules shift every year, so keep a current [social media dimensions guide](https://viewinsta.com/social-media-dimensions-guide) on the board rather than trusting the numbers in a two-year-old template.

For the reading side of design — process write-ups, critique, systems thinking — the highlighter earns its place. Mark the paragraph where a designer explains *why* the rebrand dropped the mascot, in one of six colors, with a note attached. Highlights persist on the page when you revisit and sync into your library, so the insight is retrievable separately from the 4,000-word essay wrapped around it.

## Finding "that site with the weird serifs"

Here's the retrieval problem in its designer-specific form: your memory of a reference is a description, never a URL. Marqly's semantic search matches meaning across titles, page content, and your highlights — so queries like "the article defending brutalist web design" or "portfolio that treated case studies like editorial features" pull up the right saves. It's search built for how references are remembered; [what is semantic search](/faq/what-is-semantic-search) covers the mechanics. And because AI auto-tags every save, the library develops browsable structure — motion, branding, typography — without you ever playing librarian. If you've historically fought folder sprawl, [how to organize bookmarks](/blog/how-to-organize-bookmarks) makes the case for retiring that fight entirely.

Video belongs in the reference library too. Design talks, process breakdowns, and tool walkthroughs on YouTube get an AI card with a streaming summary, key sections, and a synced transcript — and Marqly's bookmark button saves the video with its transcript attached, so the talk where someone explained their grid rationale is findable by what was said in it.

## Getting started

1. **Install the extension** — available for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari. Free signup at [app.marqly.com](https://app.marqly.com), no card required ([details on the free tier](/faq/is-marqly-free)).
2. **Import the collection you already have.** Browser bookmark HTML exports import directly, as do Pocket archives and Raindrop.io collections — wherever your references currently hibernate.
3. **Let AI tag everything.** The backlog gets auto-tagged on import, and each new save is tagged the moment you make it. Curation becomes purely additive: save what strikes you, and structure takes care of itself.

If you're comparing tools first, [the best AI bookmark managers of 2026](/blog/best-ai-bookmark-manager-2026) is a fair place to start. And an honest boundary line: Marqly is not a design asset manager — it won't replace Figma libraries or a DAM for fonts, logos, and working files, and boards are view-only when shared, not collaboratively editable. There's no offline mode and no Android app (the web app runs in Android browsers). Where it excels is the layer before the design file: catching inspiration at browse-speed and returning it at project-speed.
