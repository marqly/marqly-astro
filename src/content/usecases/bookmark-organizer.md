If you're looking for a bookmark organizer, the actual job is usually this: you have hundreds of saved links you can't find anything in, and you want that to stop. The fix is not better folders. Marqly organizes bookmarks by doing the filing for you — AI tags every save automatically — and by making retrieval work from whatever you remember: search by meaning, not by the exact words in a title.

That's the answer up front. The rest of this page is how each piece works and where the approach has limits.

## Why filing by hand keeps failing

Every bookmark folder system dies the same death. Filing takes a decision ("does this go in *Recipes* or *Weekend*?"), decisions cost effort, and effort gets skipped on busy days — which is every day. Within a month there's an "unsorted" folder bigger than all the others. And even disciplined filing breaks on retrieval, because things belong in more than one place and you search for content, not folder names.

This isn't a personal discipline problem; it's a design problem with folders themselves. We've written up the full argument in [why organizing bookmarks into folders is obsolete](/blog/stop-organizing-bookmarks-folders-obsolete). The short version: any system that requires manual filing at save time will eventually be abandoned, so the organizing has to be automatic.

## AI tags every save, so organizing just happens

In Marqly, saving is the whole workflow. One click from the browser extension (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari), and AI tags the save automatically — topic, type, whatever it's about. A recipe gets found under cooking-related tags; a JavaScript article lands under its stack. No folder decision, no tag maintenance, no weekly cleanup session you keep postponing.

Two capture features round it out:

- **Tab saver** — when a research session ends with 22 open tabs, [save them all at once](/faq/how-do-i-save-all-my-open-tabs) instead of triaging tab by tab.
- **Side-panel library** — browse and search your saves in a panel next to the page you're on, without switching tabs.

If you want intentional structure on top — a curated set for a project or a trip — boards group links and highlights deliberately, and you can share a board as a public page anyone can view without signing up. Tags are the automatic layer; boards are the curated one. More patterns in [how to organize bookmarks](/blog/how-to-organize-bookmarks).

## Search by what you remember, not what it was named

Organizing is only worth anything if retrieval works, and retrieval is where Marqly is most different from a folder tree. Semantic search finds saves by meaning: "that video about sourdough starters" surfaces the right save even if the title is "Ep. 41 — Fermentation Q&A." Search works across titles, page content, your highlights, and video transcripts.

This changes what "organized" means in practice. You don't need to remember where you put something or what it was called — you describe it the way you'd describe it to a friend. That's the retrieval mode human memory is actually good at, and it's the test a bookmark organizer should be judged on: not how tidy the shelves look, but whether a two-year-old save comes back in five seconds. Folder trees fail that test at any size; meaning-based search doesn't care how big the pile gets.

## Bring the existing mess with you

A bookmark organizer that starts from zero ignores the real problem: the 1,400 bookmarks you already have. Marqly imports:

- **Browser bookmark HTML exports** — the standard format Chrome, Firefox, Edge, and Safari all produce.
- **Pocket exports** — if your backlog lived there before it shut down.
- **Raindrop.io collections.**

Imports get AI tags on the way in. That's the moment a decade of dead folders turns back into a usable library: the links you filed under "misc" in 2019 become findable by describing them.
