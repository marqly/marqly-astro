Raindrop.io and Linkwarden both manage bookmarks; they disagree about what a bookmark is. To Raindrop it's a reference — something to file beautifully into collections and find later. To Linkwarden it's evidence — something to preserve, which is why every saved link is automatically captured as a screenshot, PDF, and readable copy, with optional push to the Wayback Machine. When the original page changes or dies, Raindrop free users hold a dead pointer; Linkwarden users still hold the page.

**Choose Raindrop.io if**

- You want the best free hosted plan in the category: unlimited bookmarks, collections, highlights, and devices without running anything.
- Polish across platforms matters — mature apps on web, Mac, iOS, and Android versus Linkwarden's mobile apps, which only launched in late 2025 and are still catching up.
- Your archiving needs are occasional: Pro at $28/year adds permanent page copies and daily backups when you're ready.

**Choose Linkwarden if**

- Link rot genuinely threatens your work — research, citations, references — and preservation must be automatic, not an upgrade.
- You want open-source ownership with a cheap hosted option: self-host the AGPL code free with full features, or pay $3/month per user billed yearly for Cloud.
- You collaborate: collections with per-user permissions make it one of the few bookmark managers genuinely built for teams.

Neither is a comfortable reading app, but Raindrop Pro now goes beyond tag suggestions: Stella can retrieve by meaning, summarize, and chat across the library when asked. Linkwarden's stronger reason to exist remains preservation rather than conversational retrieval. The split is straightforward: casual and visual collectors get more, for less effort, from Raindrop; archivists, researchers, and teams who need every saved page to still exist in ten years should pay Linkwarden's small toll, in dollars or in Docker.
