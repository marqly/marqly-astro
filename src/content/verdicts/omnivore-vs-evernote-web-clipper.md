Lead with the fact that settles it: Omnivore no longer exists as a service. Its founders announced in late October 2024 that they were joining ElevenLabs, the hosted app closed within weeks, and anything not exported by November 30, 2024 was deleted. Evernote, by contrast, is under active development by Bending Spoons — version 11 shipped in January 2026 with an AI assistant, semantic search, and AI meeting notes. The comparison that remains is philosophical: a free, open-source read-it-later app versus a commercial clipper feeding one of the oldest note platforms around.

**Choose an Omnivore fork if:**

- Free and open source is non-negotiable and you can operate a heavy self-hosted stack — PostgreSQL, Elasticsearch, and multiple microservices — on volunteer-maintained code.
- You only ever wanted a clean article queue, which Evernote was never built to be.

**Choose Evernote Web Clipper if:**

- You want clipped pages, PDFs, and screenshots searchable alongside real notes, with OCR that reads text inside images.
- You'll use the AI layer — chatting across your notes and semantic search arrived with v11.
- You clip from everywhere: the extension covers Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge, and Opera with full-page, article, selection, and annotated-screenshot modes.

The money tells the deeper story. Omnivore never charged anyone — and that free-with-no-revenue model is precisely why it could vanish overnight. Evernote sits at the other extreme: the free plan's 50-note, one-notebook, one-device cap makes it a demo, and real use costs $99 to $249.99 per year, among the priciest in the category. Ex-Omnivore users get a sustainable product this time, but they pay for the sustainability. If all you need is saving and reading articles, Evernote is overkill; if you want a durable everything-archive with serious search, it's a more honest bargain than free ever was.
