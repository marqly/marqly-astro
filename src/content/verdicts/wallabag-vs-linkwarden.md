These two share a philosophy — open-source code, free self-hosting, a cheap official cloud that funds development — but they point at different problems. Wallabag, maintained continuously since 2013, is a read-it-later app: pages get stripped to a clean article view, queued up, tagged by rules, and exported to ePub for e-readers. Linkwarden is a bookmark archive: every saved link is preserved as a screenshot, a PDF, and a readable copy, organized into nested collections you can share publicly or edit with teammates under per-user permissions.

**Choose Wallabag if:**

- You actually read what you save — distraction-free article view, offline iOS and Android apps, and annotation on selected text.
- You own an e-reader: ePub and PDF export is a signature feature.
- You want the cheapest hosted plan in the category — wallabag.it costs €11/year with a 14-day trial.

**Choose Linkwarden if:**

- Link rot worries you; the original page can die and your archived copy survives.
- You collaborate — shared collections with permissions are something Wallabag simply doesn't do.
- You want AI to help file things: optional auto-tagging, including via local models, at $3/month for cloud or free self-hosted.

Weigh the weaknesses too. Wallabag has no AI of any kind, its interface feels dated, and its parser can mangle complex, script-heavy sites — a failure mode Linkwarden sidesteps by storing the actual page in multiple formats. Linkwarden, in turn, is not a reader: there's no reading queue, no text-to-speech, no progress tracking, and its mobile apps only arrived in late 2025. Neither has a free cloud tier beyond a trial, but self-hosting costs nothing for both. Solo readers with a nightly queue should take Wallabag; researchers, hoarders, and teams building a permanent reference library should take Linkwarden.
