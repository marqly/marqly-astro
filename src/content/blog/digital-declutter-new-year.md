Here's a digital declutter you can actually finish: five passes — **tabs, bookmarks, reading backlog, subscriptions, phone** — each time-boxed, totaling about two hours, ordered by payoff so you can stop anywhere and still be better off. No 30-day monk mode, no empty-inbox theology. And because a purge without a system just rebuilds itself by February, the last step sets up the capture habit that keeps it clean.

One rule before you start: **archive, don't agonize.** Every decision in this checklist is reversible — you'll save things into a searchable archive, not delete your digital past. Speed comes from knowing nothing is truly lost.

## Pass 1: The tabs (20 minutes)

Open tabs are the highest-interest debt in your digital life: every one is an unfinished decision your brain keeps half-loaded, and one browser crash wipes the lot.

1. **Count them.** Across all windows and devices. Forty-plus is normal; own the number.
2. **Save everything worth keeping in one action.** Don't evaluate tabs one by one — that's how this pass eats the whole evening. With Marqly's extension, the tab saver captures every open tab at once into your library, auto-tagged; the whole window becomes searchable saves in seconds. No extension? Bookmark all tabs into a dated folder (`Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+D` in Chrome).
3. **Close every window.** Yes, all of them. Whatever mattered is captured; whatever wasn't captured demonstrably didn't matter — you hadn't looked at it in weeks.
4. **Adopt the ten-tab rule going forward.** When tabs pass ten, the overflow gets saved-and-closed, not kept "for later." A tab is for *right now*; a save is for later. Confusing the two is how you got here.

## Pass 2: The bookmarks (30 minutes)

Years of "I'll need this someday" across two or three browsers, none of it findable. The goal of this pass is *consolidation into one searchable place* — not building the perfect folder tree, which is a trap that has killed a thousand declutters.

1. **Export from every browser.** Each browser's bookmark manager has an export-to-HTML option (in Chrome: Bookmark manager → ⋮ → Export bookmarks). Do this even for the browser you abandoned in 2023. These files are your safety net.
2. **Prune only the obvious corpses.** Dead links, expired sales, docs for tools you no longer use, the twelve duplicates of your own homepage. Ten minutes, ruthless, done. Do not deliberate over the maybes.
3. **Consolidate into one home.** Import the HTML files into a single bookmark manager — [here's the Chrome import walkthrough](/blog/how-to-import-chrome-bookmarks-to-ai). In Marqly, imports are auto-tagged and indexed for semantic search, which means the "sort everything into folders" step you were dreading simply doesn't exist: you'll retrieve by describing what you remember, not by re-filing 900 links. (Why manual re-filing was never going to happen anyway: [the case against folder taxonomies](/blog/how-to-organize-bookmarks).)
4. **Keep the browser bar for the daily dozen.** The handful of sites you open every day stay as browser bookmarks. Everything else — the reference library — lives in the manager.

## Pass 3: The reading backlog (20 minutes)

The read-it-later queue with 400 articles isn't a reading list anymore; it's a guilt list. Same for the YouTube Watch Later pile — [that queue deserves its own rescue](/blog/save-youtube-videos-watch-later).

1. **Declare bankruptcy on the bulk.** Anything saved more than six months ago that you can't specifically remember wanting: archive it wholesale. If it becomes relevant, search will find it. You are not going to "catch up" — nobody ever has.
2. **Triage the recent hundred by headline.** Three piles, two seconds each: *actually want to read* (keep), *only need the gist* (next step), *reflex-save* (archive).
3. **Let AI summaries clear the gist pile.** For pieces you need the point of but will never read fully, an AI summary settles it in thirty seconds each — Marqly generates them for saved articles, which is how a 40-item pile becomes ten genuine reads and thirty settled summaries in one sitting.
4. **Cap the queue going forward.** A reading list you trust is short. Twenty items max; adding the twenty-first means archiving something first.

## Pass 4: The subscriptions (30 minutes)

The pass that pays actual money.

1. **Newsletters:** search your email for "unsubscribe" and sort the senders. Keep only those you *finished* reading in the last month — unsubscribe from the rest, including the good-but-unread ones. Aspiration is how the pile formed. Genuinely valuable issues can be saved to your library before the unsubscribe; keep the content, drop the recurring obligation.
2. **Paid subscriptions:** pull the last three months of bank and card statements plus your phone's subscription list (App Store / Google Play → subscriptions), and list every recurring charge. The forgotten $6–12/month apps hide in the app-store list. Cancel anything you didn't consciously use in the last 60 days. Typical haul from this pass: $30–80/month back.
3. **Free-tier accounts** (optional): note services you no longer use as you encounter them and delete the accounts — less data lying around in future breaches. Don't turn this into an archaeology project; handle them as they surface.

## Pass 5: The phone (15 minutes)

1. **Home screen: one screen.** Daily apps on screen one; everything else lives in the app library/drawer and gets opened by search. Ruthless is fine — nothing is uninstalled by being moved.
2. **Delete the dead weight:** apps unopened in six months, minus the emergencies (banking, travel, medical).
3. **Notifications: default to off.** Every app except humans-reaching-you (calls, messages) and time-critical tools loses notification rights. This five-minute block quietly outperforms everything else in this checklist for daily calm.

## Pass 6: Install the system so it doesn't rebuild (15 minutes)

Here's the honest part: **a declutter without a capture system is a subscription to decluttering.** The tabs, backlog, and bookmark sprawl all trace to one root cause — "I want to keep this" had no fast, trusted destination, so keeping meant leaving things open, piling things up, and promising to sort later.

Give it a destination:

1. **One home for keepers.** Install a bookmark manager extension in every browser you use. One click saves the page; AI tagging means no filing decision, ever — the decision-free part is what makes the habit survive.
2. **Trust retrieval, not memory.** With semantic search you find saves by describing them — "that piece about slow productivity" — so archiving stops feeling like losing things, which is what made you hoard tabs in the first place. This one habit is the seed of a genuine [second brain](/blog/how-to-build-a-second-brain), minus the elaborate setup.
3. **A weekly ten-minute sweep.** Same day each week: tabs past ten get saved-and-closed, inbox skimmed, done. Ten minutes weekly is the entire maintenance cost of everything you fixed today.
