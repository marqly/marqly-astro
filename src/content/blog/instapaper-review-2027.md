## What changed since our 2026 review

Six corrections, and the first one is ours rather than Instapaper's.

1. **We dated the price increase wrong, and named the wrong old price.** The 2026 edition said "the price doubled in 2025" and, in the pricing section, that "in 2025 Instapaper raised Premium from $2.99 to $5.99." Both are wrong. Our fact sheet records that **Premium roughly doubled in late 2023, from $3/month and $30/year** to the current $5.99/month and $59.99/year. The year was off by two and the old monthly figure by a cent that happens to be the difference between a real historical price and a number someone half-remembered. This matters because a doubling in 2023 is a settled fact about a company's pricing behaviour, while a doubling "last year" reads as a live grievance — and we published the grievance version.
2. **Our comparison table marked Marqly's offline row as "No."** It should not have. Offline reading is a **Pro feature of the web app and the iOS app**, and it is **per-device**: there is no server copy, so the offline library does not follow you between devices, and the Android app and browser extensions do not have it. The 2026 edition marked that cell "No," which understated our own product in the one row Instapaper is most famous for winning. Instapaper still wins the row — its offline reading is broader, older and includes Kindle delivery — but "No" was simply false.
3. **The Pocket import row needed precision it did not have.** We wrote "Pocket import: Yes / Yes" for Instapaper and Marqly as though those meant the same thing. They do not. Instapaper has a **direct Pocket import**. Marqly imports the **`list.csv` file from inside a Pocket export archive** — its importer reads that CSV, not the HTML preview file, which is a `<ul><li>` layout rather than the Netscape bookmark format and does not parse. Our [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity) measured exactly this on September 26, 2026: **0 of 261 items parsed from a Pocket export's HTML preview file, 43 of 43 from a Chrome bookmark export**. If you are rescuing a Pocket archive, the file you pick is the whole outcome.
4. **The platform list was incomplete.** We wrote "web, iOS, and Android, with browser extensions and a well-loved Kindle integration." The verified set is **web, iOS, Android and Kindle, with extensions for Chrome, Firefox and Safari** — and no Edge extension. Kindle is a platform, not an integration footnote, and Safari support is worth naming because a lot of rivals do not have it.
5. **Claims we dropped as unverifiable.** "Shows ads on the website" on the free plan and "ad-free website" on Premium; speed reading "up to 3x"; an email-in address; a "simple like system." None appear in our fact sheet. What the sheet does verify is **send-to-Kindle delivery, a PDF reader, speed reading, text-to-speech playlists, unlimited notes, full-text search and a permanent archive on Premium**, plus **highlights and basic notes on the free plan**.
6. **We mischaracterised the development pace.** The 2026 edition ran a weakness headed "Slow development pace" with roadmap items "planned." Our fact sheet takes a different and fairer line: Instapaper is **actively maintained by Instant Paper, Inc.**, and recent years brought a permanent archive, text-to-speech playlists, a Pocket import tool after Pocket's 2025 shutdown, and a price increase — **"signs of an ongoing, sustainable business rather than an abandoned app."** The honest criticism is not that development is slow. It is that every addition has been on the **consumption** side — reading, listening, archiving — and none on the **organisation or retrieval** side, which is where the category moved.

What we omitted entirely and should not have: Instapaper has a **documented developer API** supporting third-party clients, and its **highlights and notes can sync out to services like Readwise**. Both are real portability wins, and the 2026 edition credited neither.

## What is Instapaper?

Instapaper is the original read-it-later app. **Marco Arment launched it in 2008**; it passed through betaworks and then Pinterest before going independent again in **2018 under Instant Paper, Inc.**, which still runs it. When Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025, Instapaper became the longest-running service in the category by a wide margin — a genuine argument in its favour if you are deciding where to put a decade of saved articles, and the reason it added a direct Pocket import.

The concept has not changed in eighteen years. Save an article from anywhere; Instapaper strips the ads, popups and layout noise and leaves clean text in typography that has been tuned since the Obama administration; the queue syncs to your phone, tablet, Kindle and the web, and downloads for offline reading.

It is deliberately minimal, and the minimalism is a position rather than a gap. There is no recommendation engine, no automatic organisation, no intelligence layer, no social surface and no public sharing. You save, you read, you highlight, you file into a folder if you feel like it. In 2027, when almost every rival ships an AI feature of some kind, Instapaper is the last tool in the category that will simply leave you alone.

That is either the reason to use it or the reason not to, and almost nothing else about the product will decide the question for you.

## Key features

- **The reader.** Instapaper's parsed article view is still the benchmark: excellent typography, tasteful defaults, dark mode, adjustable fonts and spacing, and pagination that reads like a book rather than a scroll. Eighteen years of polish shows. If you read thousands of words a day, this is the feature that matters and nobody does it better.
- **One of the most reliable parsers in the business.** Eighteen years of edge cases have been thrown at it. Complex and paywalled pages still occasionally get mangled — our fact sheet records that — but the failure rate is lower than in younger apps, and the graceful handling of a broken layout is a real engineering achievement that users underrate because they only notice it when it fails.
- **Offline reading.** Articles download to your device automatically, so a flight or a subway tunnel does not interrupt you. This remains one of Instapaper's most practical advantages, and plenty of newer, smarter tools still cannot do it.
- **Send-to-Kindle (Premium).** Scheduled delivery of batches of saved articles to an e-ink device. Almost nobody else builds this, and for Kindle readers it can justify Premium on its own.
- **A PDF reader (Premium).** Distinct from saving as PDF — Instapaper does not produce PDFs of your saves, but Premium subscribers can read PDFs inside it.
- **Highlights and notes.** Highlight passages and attach notes. The free plan **caps notes at five per month**, which is tight enough to function as a nudge toward Premium; Premium removes the cap. Highlights and notes can **sync out to services like Readwise**, which is the reason a lot of serious annotators run Instapaper as the capture layer.
- **Full-text search and a permanent archive (Premium).** Premium searches the full text of everything you have saved and keeps a permanent copy of every article, so your library survives dead links. Both work well. Both are keyword-literal rather than meaning-based, and both are gated behind a subscription that free accounts cannot sample.
- **Speed reading and text-to-speech playlists (Premium).** A tap-to-speed-read mode and machine-narrated audio with playlists on mobile. The narration is decent for commutes and trails dedicated listening apps such as Matter.
- **Saving everywhere that matters.** Extensions for Chrome, Firefox and Safari, share-sheet saving on iOS and Android, and Kindle delivery. No Edge extension.
- **A documented developer API** supporting third-party clients — older than most of the category and still maintained.

What is absent is a coherent philosophy rather than a backlog. There is **no AI auto-tagging, no AI summaries, no semantic search, no chat with your library and no YouTube tooling**. There is **no public sharing** of any kind. There is **no save-as-PDF**. There is **no one-click save-all-tabs**. Organisation is **folders** — not tags, not automatic collections, not a smart queue — and there is **no browser-bookmark HTML import**, so arriving from Chrome or Firefox means arriving via a Pocket export or an intermediate tool.

The pricing history, corrected: **Premium roughly doubled in late 2023, from $3/month and $30/year.** The current prices have therefore held for about three years, which is a materially different fact from "they doubled last year." Nine-plus years of stability preceded the increase and the increase deserves the criticism it got at the time — our fact sheet notes it arrived "without major new features." But the value question in 2027 is not whether the 2023 increase was justified. It is whether $59.99/year is competitive *now*, and the arithmetic below says it is more competitive than we implied.

Two structural notes on the ladder.

**The free tier is a real tier.** Unlimited saving with no card and no expiry is more generous than Readwise Reader, which has no permanent free plan at all, and more useful than Karakeep Cloud's ten-bookmark sample. What it is not is searchable: the single most important limit on the free plan is that **you cannot full-text search your own saves**. You can scroll, you can browse folders, and you can remember. That is a genuine paywall on retrieval rather than on volume, and it is the one that will annoy you.

**Premium buys search, permanence and audio — and nothing organisational.** Read that list again with an eye to what is missing. There is no tier at any price where Instapaper tags something for you, summarises something for you, or finds an article because you described it. The money buys better consumption and better retention. It does not buy retrieval by meaning.

## Durability: the strongest survival record in the category

This is the section our 2026 edition did not write, and in 2027 it is arguably Instapaper's best argument.

The category's recent record is grim. **Omnivore shut down in November 2024** after its team was acqui-hired by ElevenLabs; users had until **November 30, 2024** to export before deletion, and the free-with-no-revenue model is the usual suspect. **Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025** — export access and the API disabled on **November 12, 2025**, data deletion beginning then, Premium subscribers refunded pro-rata. Both are marked `shutdown` in our data layer. Pocket in particular had been the default recommendation for a decade and was owned by a foundation, not a startup, and it still went away.

Against that, Instapaper's record is exceptional:

**Eighteen years of continuous operation.** 2008 to 2026, through three owners — Arment, betaworks, Pinterest — and independent again since 2018 under Instant Paper, Inc. Ownership changes are the moment a service usually dies. Instapaper survived three.

**A revenue model that has existed since the beginning.** Premium has been the business since 2009-ish, and the fact sheet reads the 2023 price increase, the permanent archive, the TTS playlists and the Pocket import tool as **"signs of an ongoing, sustainable business rather than an abandoned app."** It is not free-with-no-revenue, which is the model that killed Omnivore. It is not a strategic accessory to a larger business, which is what Pinterest was, and which is the riskiest posture of all — accessories get cut when strategy changes.

**Export available on the free plan.** This is the detail that matters most and gets the least credit. Account settings on the web export your saved articles as an **HTML file or CSV**, and **exports are not gated behind Premium**. The HTML export is a standard bookmarks format that most read-it-later and bookmarking apps can import directly. Add a documented developer API, and highlight-and-note sync to services like Readwise, and Instapaper has three separate ways out.

**The honest caveat.** Export gets your list of links out. It does not get your **permanent archive** out — the copies of articles Premium keeps against link rot are the one asset that does not survive leaving, because they are the thing you are paying for. And the HTML export carries URLs, not highlights: if a decade of annotation is your real library, the Readwise sync path is the one to test *before* you need it, not after. If you want your annotations in a portable file today, the [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity) is a useful reality check on what any migration actually preserves.

If durability is your top criterion and you want the stronger version — where the archive itself is yours rather than rented — the open-source route is [Linkwarden](/blog/linkwarden-review-2027) or [Karakeep](/blog/karakeep-review-2027), both free to self-host with standard-format exports. Our [Pocket alternatives guide for 2027](/blog/best-pocket-alternatives-2027) walks that decision.

## Strengths

- **The best pure reading experience in the category.** Typography and parsing quality that newer apps still have not matched, refined over eighteen years.
- **Offline reading that just works.** Save on Wi-Fi, read anywhere, on iPhone, iPad and Android.
- **Send-to-Kindle.** The e-ink crowd has no better friend, and almost nobody else builds it.
- **The strongest survival record we cover.** Eighteen years, three owners, zero data disasters, export on the free plan. After watching Pocket and Omnivore die, that is worth real money.
- **A genuinely usable free plan.** Unlimited saving, folders, offline reading and highlights at $0 with no card.
- **Cheap for what it is.** $59.99/year is below Marqly Pro and half what Readwise Reader Full costs.
- **One of the most reliable article parsers available**, with eighteen years of edge cases behind it.
- **Real portability.** HTML or CSV export ungated by plan, a documented developer API, and highlight sync to Readwise.
- **It will leave you alone.** No recommendations, no engagement mechanics, no social layer, no AI nudges. Increasingly rare and increasingly valuable.

## Weaknesses

- **Free accounts cannot search their own saves.** Not "search is limited" — full-text search is Premium, and on the free plan you browse folders and remember. This is a paywall on retrieval rather than on volume, and it is the single worst limit in the product.
- **Even Premium search is literal.** Keyword matching over article text. If you cannot recall the words, you cannot find the article. There is no meaning-based retrieval at any price.
- **Organisation is folders, full stop.** No tags, no automatic organisation, nothing done for you. Every article lands in a pile unless you file it by hand — the pattern that turns read-it-later apps into [read-it-never graveyards](/blog/best-pocket-alternatives-2027).
- **No AI layer of any kind.** No summaries to triage a backlog, no auto-tagging, no semantic retrieval, no chat with your library. The additions of recent years — TTS playlists, speed reading, the permanent archive — all polish consumption. None touch organisation or recall, which are the two problems that actually kill saved-article habits, as we break down in the [AI bookmark manager guide](/blog/best-ai-bookmark-manager-2027).
- **Notes capped at five per month on the free plan.** Tight enough to feel like a nudge, and a hard wall for anyone who annotates as they read.
- **No browser-bookmark HTML import.** Arriving from Chrome, Firefox or Safari means arriving via a Pocket export or an intermediate tool.
- **No public sharing.** Nothing you save can be published or shown to anyone. For a teacher, a book club, a research group or a newsletter writer, that is disqualifying on its own.
- **No Edge extension.** Chrome, Firefox and Safari.
- **Parsing occasionally mangles complex or paywalled pages.** Better than most, not perfect.
- **The wrong shape for non-articles.** Videos, images, reference links and PDFs beyond the Premium reader all sit awkwardly in a queue designed for prose.

## Who should skip Instapaper

Real disqualifiers, in the order people hit them:

- **You save more than you read.** Instapaper will organise your guilt beautifully. It will not summarise the pile, will not tag it, and will not find the half-remembered piece from March. If the backlog is the problem, this is the wrong tool and no amount of typography fixes it.
- **You need to search on the free plan.** You cannot. Full-text search over your own saves is Premium. This is the most common reason people bounce.
- **You want to search by meaning, at any price.** Keyword matching is the whole retrieval surface, on Premium too.
- **You share anything with anyone.** No public pages, no shared collections, no way to show a list to a class, a book club or a colleague. If you teach, our [bookmark managers for teachers guide](/blog/best-bookmark-managers-for-teachers-2027) explains why a view-only public page decides this.
- **You save things that are not articles.** Videos, images, reference links, tools, products. The queue is built for prose and everything else sits awkwardly in it.
- **You live in Microsoft Edge.** No Edge extension.
- **You are migrating browser bookmarks directly.** There is no browser-bookmark HTML import; you need a Pocket export or an intermediate tool.
- **You annotate heavily and will not pay.** Five notes per month on Free, and that is the wall.
- **You want a reading workflow — a queue, progress, spaced repetition.** Instapaper has none of that machinery. Readwise Reader is built around it.

And who should *not* skip it: long-form readers who want the best typography available and a distraction-free queue; Kindle owners, for whom scheduled delivery alone can justify Premium; commuters and travellers who need offline reading that has worked since before the App Store had a dark mode; ex-Pocket users who want the closest like-for-like landing spot and value an eighteen-year track record over a feature list; and anyone who has decided that the last thing they want from a reading app is another intelligence layer telling them what to read next. Inside that profile Instapaper is the best tool in the category, it is cheaper than we said last year, and it is the one most likely to still exist in 2035.

## Verdict

Instapaper in 2027 is a lovely, trustworthy, single-purpose tool — and, correcting ourselves, a cheaper one than our 2026 edition implied. Premium roughly doubled in **late 2023**, not last year, from $3/month and $30/year to **$5.99/month or $59.99/year**, which means the current prices have held for about three years and now sit below Marqly Pro and at half what Readwise Reader Full costs over five years. The free tier is genuinely usable: unlimited saving, folders, offline reading, no card. The reading experience is the best in the category and the parser is the most reliable. Eighteen years and three owners with zero data disasters is the strongest durability record we cover, and export is available on the free plan.

The limitations are structural rather than fixable-by-roadmap. Free accounts cannot full-text search their own saves. Search is keyword-literal even on Premium. Notes cap at five a month on Free. Organisation is folders. There is no public sharing, no Edge extension, no browser-HTML import, and no AI of any kind — no summaries, no auto-tagging, no semantic retrieval, no chat. Every recent addition has been on the consumption side. None has touched the two problems that actually kill saved-article habits: filing and finding.

Keep Instapaper if you read more than you save. If you save more than you read, fix the retrieval side first — [Marqly](https://app.marqly.com) is free to start with no card, imports your existing library through the [Migration Center](/migrate) following our [Instapaper guide](/migrate/instapaper), and searches it by what you remember rather than by what you typed.
