Raindrop.io is still the best traditional bookmark manager you can get, and in 2027 the free plan is still the single best deal in the category: **unlimited bookmarks, unlimited collections and unlimited devices, on every platform, forever, with no card and no expiry.** Pro costs **$28/year direct** or **$3.25/month**, and adds full-text search, permanent copies of every page, annotations, reminders and daily backups. There is exactly one real ceiling, and it has not moved since we first reviewed the product: **Raindrop's search matches keywords, not meaning**, so finding a save you half-remember is still your job rather than the app's.

We publish this review on Marqly's blog and Marqly competes with Raindrop, so let us be plain about the shape of it. Raindrop is an excellent product, this review says so repeatedly, and if what you want is a beautiful, keyword-searchable library at the lowest possible price, you can stop reading now and go install it. The rest of this page covers what changed since our 2026 edition, what Pro genuinely adds, what Stella is and is not, whether the company will still be here in 2029, and the specific cases where Raindrop is the wrong pick.

## What changed since our 2026 review

Seven corrections and additions, three of which change what a reader of the old edition would believe.

1. **We quoted the wrong price channel.** The 2026 edition led with "$27.99/year (≈$2.33/month) or $2.99/month." Those are the **iOS App Store** numbers. The direct price on raindrop.io is **$28/year or $3.25/month**. The difference matters in both directions: the App Store is genuinely cheaper if you pay monthly ($2.99 versus $3.25) and marginally cheaper annually ($27.99 versus $28). Neither figure is wrong, but only one of them is the web price, and the 2026 edition presented the App Store price as if it were universal.
2. **We over-claimed the AI.** The 2026 edition said Pro includes "an AI assistant that can answer questions about saved content" and listed "AI Q&A over your saves" as a Raindrop capability. Our current fact sheet records **chat with saves: absent**, and its cons list is explicit that Raindrop's AI is "limited to save-time tag and collection suggestions (Stella) on Pro; no automatic tagging across saves, no summaries, no chat, no YouTube features." That was our error and it is corrected below.
3. **The AI has a name.** It is **Stella**, and it operates at the moment of saving, not afterwards.
4. **The free plan includes unlimited devices.** We listed bookmarks and collections in 2026 and left devices out.
5. **Linux belongs in the platform list.** The 2026 edition named Mac, Windows, iOS and Android in its platform paragraph and mentioned Linux only once further down. Raindrop ships a native Linux app.
6. **Four Pro claims in the 2026 edition do not survive re-verification.** We listed a **broken-link finder**, **10 GB/month of Pro uploads**, **priority support**, and full-text search covering "**EPUBs and even YouTube transcripts**." The current fact sheet verifies a **duplicate finder** and daily backups, gives no Pro upload figure at all, does not list priority support, and scopes full-text search to **saved pages and PDFs** while recording **no YouTube features on any plan**. We have removed all four claims rather than repeat details we cannot source, and narrowed the search scope to what is verified.
7. **No offline mode, stated explicitly.** Saved pages are cached; there is no dedicated offline reading mode. The 2026 edition implied this without saying it.

What has not changed is the important part: the free plan is still unlimited where it counts, Pro is still the cheapest serious paid tier in the market, and search is still keyword matching.

## What is Raindrop.io?

Raindrop.io is an all-in-one bookmark manager in continuous development since **2013**, built and run by a very small team around its original developer. It saves anything with a URL — articles, videos, PDFs, images, whole files — and organises it into nested collections with cover art, tags, filters and several view modes.

It runs everywhere: **web, macOS, Windows, Linux, iOS and Android**, with extensions for **Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari**. That coverage matters more than it sounds, because Raindrop is one of the very few premium-feeling bookmark tools with first-class native apps on both Android and Linux.

The philosophy is a *library*, not a reading queue. Raindrop is closer to a personal Pinterest crossed with a filing cabinet than to a read-it-later app, though it does keep permanent copies of pages and supports highlights and annotations. If your mental model is "a shelf I curate," this is the tool. If your mental model is "an inbox I empty," it is not.

## Key features

**Collections, tags and filters.** The organisational core, and the best in the business. Nested collections with custom icons and covers, multi-tag support, filters by type, domain and date. If you genuinely enjoy curating a structured library — and a surprising number of people do — nothing else gives you this much tooling for free.

**Save everything, from anywhere.** Articles, videos, images, PDFs, music links and file uploads, with **100 MB/month on the free plan**. The browser extensions are fast, there is a **tab saver** for grabbing every open tab at once, and the share-sheet integration on iOS and Android is solid. Nothing in this category swallows a wider range of material with less friction.

**Highlights and annotations.** You can highlight text on saved pages on any plan — the free tier includes **up to 3 highlights per bookmark**, and Pro unlocks **unlimited highlights in 4 colours** plus notes attached to them. One structural caveat: highlights live inside Raindrop's copy of the page rather than persisting on the live web page when you revisit it.

**Permanent copies and full-text search (Pro).** Pro keeps an archived copy of every page you save, so the library survives link rot — the failure mode that quietly destroys read-it-later collections over years. Pro's full-text search then covers the content of saved pages and PDFs, rather than only titles, tags and descriptions. Within a keyword paradigm, it is excellent.

**Sharing and collaboration.** Public collections, collaborator-shared collections, and clean public pages that anyone can open. Sharing is not a Pro gate, which makes Raindrop one of the better collaboration stories among personal bookmark tools.

**Stella (Pro).** AI-suggested tags and collections at save time. Useful, and the honest scope of Raindrop's AI. More in its own section below.

## What Raindrop does well

- **The free plan is the best in the category, and it is not a trial in disguise.** Unlimited bookmarks, unlimited collections, unlimited devices, three highlights per bookmark, import and export, sharing, every app and every extension. Most competitors gate one of those; Raindrop gates none.
- **Cross-platform coverage including Android and Linux.** Web, Mac, Windows, Linux, iOS, Android, Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari. Very few tools match that spread.
- **Media breadth.** It takes files, images, video links and PDFs where read-it-later tools mostly want articles.
- **Pro is cheap and honest.** $28/year for permanent page copies, full-text search and daily backups is the best price-to-capability ratio in the market.
- **A documented API and 2,600+ integrations.** Zapier, IFTTT and direct API access, on a product whose free tier already works.
- **Thirteen years of continuity with export always on.** As custodians of other people's libraries go, that is a long and clean record.

## Where Raindrop falls short

- **Search matches keywords, not meaning.** The big one, and the reason this review exists. More below.
- **Organisation is manual by default.** Collections and tags are powerful, and you are the one filing. Stella suggests at save time; there is no automatic tagging sweep across an existing library and no AI summaries to triage a backlog.
- **Full-text search and annotations are Pro-gated.** Beyond three highlights per bookmark, and beyond title-and-tag matching, you pay. At $28/year that is a mild objection rather than a real one.
- **The reading experience is basic.** It is a library, not a reader, and there is **no dedicated offline mode** on any platform.
- **Small-team risk.** The same tiny team that makes Raindrop impressively focused also means priorities move at a small team's pace. Stella arriving late, and Pro-only, is the clearest example.

## What Pro actually adds, line by line

Because "$28/year" is easy to nod at without checking what you are buying:

- **Full-text search** across the content of saved pages and PDFs. On free you search titles, tags and descriptions. This is the single biggest functional jump.
- **Permanent page copies** — an archived snapshot of every save, so a deleted or paywalled article does not take your library entry with it.
- **Unlimited highlights in 4 colours, with notes.** Free stops at 3 highlights per bookmark and has no annotations.
- **Stella** — AI tag and collection suggestions at save time.
- **Reminders** on saved items.
- **Duplicate finder** across the library.
- **Daily backups** of your library, on top of the manual export everyone gets.

What Pro does *not* add, which is worth stating because marketing copy in this category blurs it: no AI summaries of saved articles, no chat with your library, no semantic search, no YouTube features of any kind, no offline reading mode, and no automatic tagging sweep across an existing collection.

At $28/year, that is still the best value in the market. Over five years, Pro costs $140 direct — less than two years of most competitors' paid tiers, and under a quarter of five years of Readwise Reader at $119.88/year.

## Stella: what Raindrop's AI does and does not do

Stella is the name of Raindrop's AI layer, and its scope is narrow enough to describe completely: **when you save something on Pro, Stella suggests tags and suggests which collection it belongs in.** You accept, edit or ignore.

That is a real and useful feature. Tag discipline is the thing that makes a keyword-searchable library work, and tag discipline is also the thing people abandon in month three. A suggestion at the moment of capture is well-placed, because it costs nothing and it keeps the taxonomy from drifting.

What Stella is not, and our 2026 edition got this wrong:

- **It is not an assistant you can ask questions.** Chat with your saves is recorded as absent in our fact sheet.
- **It is not automatic tagging.** It suggests; it does not sweep your existing library and re-file it.
- **It is not summarisation.** There are no AI summaries of saved articles on any plan.
- **It does not touch YouTube.** No summary, no chat, no transcript card on the watch page. Our fact sheet records no YouTube features on any Raindrop plan, which is worth knowing if video is a meaningful share of what you save.
- **It does not change retrieval.** Stella improves the quality of your tags, and better tags improve keyword search. The search itself still matches words.

If you want AI applied to a library after the fact — bulk re-tagging, summaries, questions across everything you saved — that is a different product category, and our [AI bookmark manager guide](/blog/best-ai-bookmark-manager-2027) maps it.

## Search is the ceiling

Here is the concrete failure, because it is easier to feel than to describe. You remember saving "that article about why open offices kill focus." The actual title was "The war on deep work," and the phrase "open office" appears once, in the middle of paragraph nine. On the free plan you search titles, tags and descriptions, and you get nothing. On Pro, full-text search finds it — because the words are literally in the document. But if you remember the *argument* rather than the vocabulary — "the one about productivity and noise" — no amount of full-text indexing helps, because there is no string in the document to match.

That is the ceiling. Raindrop's answer is good organisation, and good organisation depends on you predicting, at save time, what words future-you will search with. Thirteen years of product development have made that prediction as easy as it can be. It is still a prediction.

Past a few hundred saves, retrieval becomes the bottleneck for exactly this reason, and we have written about the mechanics in our [Marqly vs Raindrop comparison](/compare/marqly-vs-raindrop). The honest test is not a benchmark — it is whether you have ever failed to find something you knew you saved. If never, keep Raindrop. If weekly, the ceiling is already pressing on you.

## The API and 2,600+ integrations

Underrated, and absent from our 2026 edition. Raindrop has a **documented open API** and works with **2,600+ integrations** through services like Zapier and IFTTT.

In practice this means: save to Raindrop from a spreadsheet, from an RSS pipeline, from a read-later queue, from a form, or from your own script. Push new bookmarks into Slack or Notion. Run a nightly job that exports the library and commits it somewhere. For automation-minded users this is a capability we simply do not offer — there is no public API on any Marqly plan, and no self-hosting either — and for an integration-heavy workflow it is a strong argument for Raindrop even if you would otherwise look elsewhere.

## Durability: thirteen years and one small team

Pocket shut down on **July 8, 2025**. Omnivore shut down in **November 2024**. Two of the most-recommended saving tools in the world disappeared inside eight months, and one of them was free with no revenue model at all. So "will this still exist" is now a legitimate evaluation criterion rather than a paranoid one, and Raindrop should be assessed on it honestly.

**In its favour:** continuous development since 2013, a subscription business with real paying customers, a free tier that is affordable to run because it is not subsidising an AI bill, and export available on every plan from the beginning. Nothing about Raindrop resembles the free-with-no-revenue structure that made Omnivore's shutdown possible.

**Against it:** a very small team, which is a single point of failure in a way that a large-company product is not — though note that Pocket was owned by Mozilla and still closed. And the AI layer arrived late and stayed narrow, which is what a small team looks like when it is choosing sustainability over feature races.

**The portability answer is strong.** Raindrop exports your bookmarks as **HTML or CSV on the free plan**, both of which nearly every competing manager can import. Pro adds automatic daily backups on top. There is no lock-in, and a full export is a fast operation rather than a support ticket. If you ever need to move, you move with everything.

That last point is the reason we recommend Raindrop even to people who will end up elsewhere: as a *first* landing spot for an orphaned library, its combination of unlimited free storage and clean export is hard to beat.

## Raindrop in 2027: where the ex-Pocket libraries landed

The framing of this review has changed, and the change is worth naming. In 2024 a Raindrop review was about whether to switch from Pocket. In 2027 there is no Pocket to switch from.

Mozilla turned the service off on **July 8, 2025**, disabled export access and the Pocket API on **November 12, 2025**, and says remaining data was queued for permanent deletion from that date. Premium subscribers received prorated refunds. Source: [Mozilla's Pocket shutdown notice](https://support.mozilla.org/en-US/kb/future-of-pocket).

So in 2027 the typical Raindrop onboarding is one of two things: a **rescue**, where someone exported a Pocket archive in time and is now importing the CSV from it, or a **fresh start**, where someone never exported and is rebuilding from their browser bookmarks and whatever they can find. Raindrop handles the first well — it imports from Pocket, from browser bookmark HTML and from most competing services, on the free plan.

Two things to know if you are in the rescue group. First, a Pocket export contains links and metadata, not article text, so every importer re-saves the URLs and rebuilds from the live page: **anything deleted or paywalled since 2025 will not come back**, which is an argument for importing sooner rather than later. Second, importers generally do not preserve original save dates — items take the import date — and none of them bring back highlights, because the archive never contained them. We measured what actually survives a real migration in the [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity); our [Pocket alternatives guide for 2027](/blog/best-pocket-alternatives-2027) ranks the landing spots.

## Who should skip Raindrop

Real disqualifiers, not hedged ones:

- **Your actual problem is finding things.** If retrieval is why you are shopping, no amount of Pro full-text search changes the paradigm. Skip it.
- **You want the app to do the organising.** Stella suggests at save time. Nothing re-files a library you already have.
- **You read offline.** There is no offline mode; pages are cached, which is not the same thing.
- **You want a reader, not a shelf.** RSS-style triage, newsletter inboxes, EPUB libraries and spaced-repetition review are Readwise Reader's job, not Raindrop's.
- **You want AI summaries or to chat with your library.** Neither exists on any Raindrop plan.
- **You want anything from a saved video.** No summary, no transcript card, no chat on the watch page — our fact sheet records no YouTube features on any Raindrop plan.
- **You want automation *and* semantic retrieval together.** Raindrop has the API and not the retrieval; no single tool in our data has both.

And who should not skip it: deliberate organisers who enjoy maintaining a structured visual library, anyone on Mac, Windows or Linux who wants a native desktop app, collectors of mixed media rather than articles, students and budget-first users who need a free tier that does not run out, and ex-Pocket users holding an archive who want a proven, exportable home while they work out what they actually need. ## Verdict

Raindrop.io is the best traditional bookmark manager available in 2027, and its free plan remains the single best deal in the category — unlimited bookmarks, collections and devices on ten platforms with no expiry and no card. Pro at $28/year direct adds the four things worth paying for: full-text search, permanent page copies, unlimited annotations, and daily backups. Its limitations are the things that keep it traditional. Search matches words instead of meaning, organisation ultimately depends on you, Stella is a save-time suggestion engine rather than an assistant, there is no offline mode, and a very small team sets the pace.

If none of those bite you, Raindrop is close to perfect and you should not pay more. If one of them does, pair its library with — or replace it with — a tool built around retrieval.
