The PARA method — Tiago Forte's system of **Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives** — works for bookmarks the same way it works for files and notes: you file every link by *how actionable it is*, not what topic it covers. It's genuinely the best manual system for bookmarks, and this guide shows you a working setup in five steps. It's also honest about the second half of the story: PARA assumes a human files every item, and that assumption collapses somewhere around your thirtieth save of the week. The fix isn't abandoning PARA — it's pairing it with AI tagging and semantic search, and we'll show exactly where to draw the line.

## What PARA is, in one minute

From *Building a Second Brain*: every piece of information you keep belongs in one of four buckets, ordered by actionability.

1. **Projects** — short-term efforts with a goal and an end date. "Launch the newsletter," "Kitchen renovation," "Q4 hiring round."
2. **Areas** — ongoing responsibilities with a standard to maintain, no end date. Health, finances, team management, the product you own.
3. **Resources** — topics you're interested in but not acting on. Typography, barbecue technique, prompt engineering.
4. **Archives** — anything inactive from the other three. Finished projects, lapsed interests.

The insight that makes PARA different from every topic-folder scheme: **you file by "what will this help me do?"** — not "what is this about?" A great article on negotiation goes in *Projects → Q4 hiring round* if that's why you saved it, not in a "Negotiation" topic folder where you'll never look while hiring. Information organized by action gets used; information organized by subject gets stored. If you've ever built a beautiful folder tree and then never opened it, [that's the failure PARA is designed against](/blog/how-to-organize-bookmarks).

## Step 1: Create the four top-level buckets

In whatever tool holds your links — browser folders, a bookmark manager, Marqly boards — create exactly four containers: `1 Projects`, `2 Areas`, `3 Resources`, `4 Archives`. The number prefixes keep them sorted by actionability. Resist the urge to add a fifth. "Misc" is where PARA goes to die.

## Step 2: List your active projects — before filing anything

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that makes PARA work. Write down every project you're *actively* working on right now: things with an outcome and a rough deadline. Most people land between 10 and 15. Create one subfolder per project under Projects.

Two rules:

- If you can't name the outcome, it's not a project — it's an area or a resource.
- If you haven't touched it in a month, it's not active — it starts in Archives.

## Step 3: Add areas and resources sparingly

Under **Areas**, one subfolder per ongoing responsibility — the things that would have consequences if you ignored them for a quarter. Under **Resources**, one subfolder per genuine interest. Be stingy: every folder you create is a decision you'll have to re-make on every future save. Ten resource folders you actually recognize beat forty precise ones you have to think about.

## Step 4: File new bookmarks with the actionability question

From now on, every save gets one question: **"Which active project does this move forward?"**

- Clear answer → file it in that project folder.
- No project, but it serves a responsibility → the area folder.
- Neither, but genuinely interesting → resources.
- Can't honestly place it → don't save it. (PARA's quiet superpower is making you notice how much you save out of reflex.)

Don't backfill your existing thousand bookmarks into PARA on day one — that's a weekend you'll never spend. Dump them all into Archives and pull items out when a project actually needs them. Forte's own advice for files applies doubly to bookmarks.

## Step 5: Run the weekly sweep and archive ruthlessly

PARA is maintained, not built. Once a week, ten minutes:

- Move links from finished or stalled projects into Archives.
- Re-file anything you dropped in the wrong bucket during the week.
- Kill projects that stopped being real. (The list shrinking is the system working.)

When a project ends, its whole folder moves to Archives intact — you never delete, so nothing is lost, but your active space stays small enough to trust.

## Where PARA breaks down for bookmarks

Now the part most PARA-for-bookmarks guides won't tell you. PARA was designed for documents and notes — dozens of items a month, each obviously tied to your work. Bookmarks arrive differently: fast, in volume, mid-scroll, and mostly *not* attached to an active project. At that intake rate, four cracks open up:

1. **The filing tax stops being paid.** Every save demands a category decision. At five saves a week that's fine; at thirty, you start deferring — "I'll file it later" — and PARA silently degrades into one big unfiled inbox with nice labels on top.
2. **Links refuse to pick one bucket.** A deep guide to pricing strategy is relevant to your current launch (project), your role (area), and your long-term interest (resource). Folders force one choice; whichever you pick, future-you looks in the other two first.
3. **Resources is where links go to be forgotten.** For notes, Resources gets re-mined by future projects. Bookmarks are different — out of sight, zero recall. Six months on, `Resources/Typography` holds 80 links you have no memory of saving.
4. **Retrieval depends on remembering your own filing decision.** PARA retrieval assumes you recall *where* you put a thing. For a link you filed in 40 seconds, eight months ago, under a project that's now archived? You won't. You'll end up re-googling something you already own — the universal failure mode of [manual bookmark filing at scale](/blog/stop-organizing-bookmarks-folders-obsolete).

None of this means PARA is wrong. It means PARA's manual layer has a natural capacity — roughly the 10–20% of saves attached to active work — and forcing 100% of your intake through it is what makes people quit the whole system by March.

## The hybrid: PARA structure on top, AI underneath

The setup that holds up at real volume keeps PARA for what it's uniquely good at and hands the rest to automation:

- **Auto-tagging replaces filing for the long tail.** In Marqly, every save is tagged by AI at capture — no decision, no deferred inbox. The filing tax drops to zero for the 80% of saves that were never going to earn a manual decision, and crack #1 closes.
- **Tags let one link live in many contexts.** The pricing guide carries its own descriptors instead of being trapped in a single folder — crack #2 closes.
- **Semantic search replaces remembering where you filed.** You retrieve by describing what you remember — "that article on pricing psychology with the SaaS examples" — and it surfaces regardless of which bucket it's in, or whether it's in any bucket at all. Cracks #3 and #4 close, because retrieval no longer routes through your memory of a months-old filing decision.
- **PARA survives as the action layer.** Keep a board per active project — Marqly boards work naturally as Projects folders, and you can share one as a public page when the project has collaborators. Pull links *into* project boards when work needs them; let AI handle everything outside that.

You still get PARA's real gift — a small, current, trustworthy view of what you're working on — without the clerical job that kills it. That combination, a light manual structure over an automatically-searchable base, is the practical core of [building a second brain that you'll still be using next year](/blog/how-to-build-a-second-brain).

## The bottom line

Set up PARA's four buckets and a board per active project — that part earns its keep for anyone. Just don't sign up to hand-file every link you'll ever save; that's the version of PARA that ends in a guilty backlog. File the working 20% by action, let [AI tag and index the rest](https://app.marqly.com), and search by meaning when you need something back. Structure where structure pays, automation everywhere else.
