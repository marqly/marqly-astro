## What is Linkwarden?

Linkwarden is an open-source, self-hostable bookmark manager built around a blunt observation: the web rots. Links break, pages get rewritten, publications shut down, and a bookmark to a dead page is a note that says "you used to know something." Linkwarden's answer is to archive everything automatically, in several formats, the moment you save it — and optionally to push a copy to the Internet Archive so there is a version nobody can delete.

The project is licensed **AGPL-3.0**, has grown past **19,000 GitHub stars**, and is developed in the open with a steady release rhythm. You can self-host it with Docker, in which case every feature is included at no cost, or pay for the official Cloud at linkwarden.app. The AGPL matters more than it sounds: it is the licence that keeps a self-hosted instance feature-complete rather than feature-crippled, which is the trick a lot of "open core" projects use to push you into a subscription.

The second thing to know is that Linkwarden is genuinely **collaborative** in a category that mostly is not. Collections can be shared with teammates with per-member permissions, or published as public pages anyone can view without an account. For a research group, a content team, a classroom or a shared reading list, that is the second superpower — and the reason a school or a small organisation might pick Linkwarden over a personal bookmark app.

Development is funded by the Cloud subscription, which is a sustainable model in a category where two of the best-loved free tools died inside eight months.

## Key features

- **Multi-format page preservation.** The headline feature, and the reason the product exists. Every saved link is captured as a **screenshot, a PDF and a readable text copy**, and Linkwarden can also submit the page to the **Wayback Machine**. Archived copies can be downloaded. Years from now, when a share of your links have rotted, your library still opens.
- **Collections, tags and real collaboration.** Links organise into nested collections with sub-collections, plus tags. Collections can be shared with teammates with per-user permissions or published publicly. This is executed properly rather than bolted on, and it is the feature most rivals treat as an afterthought.
- **Optional AI tagging, including local models.** Linkwarden can auto-tag new saves using AI, and it supports **local models**, so self-hosters can keep the automation on their own hardware. Note the scope precisely: **it tags**. There are no AI summaries, no chat with your library and no semantic retrieval layer. The AI files things; it does not help you get them back out.
- **Full-text search across archived content.** Search reaches into what was archived, not just titles and tags — which is more than most bookmark managers offer and less than a semantic index. It is keyword retrieval over a bigger haystack.
- **Highlights and annotation notes.** Present, and useful for marking up what you saved rather than only filing it.
- **Documented REST API with access tokens.** Integrations, scripting and full programmatic export are all first-class rather than reverse-engineered.
- **Official iOS and Android apps.** Launched late 2025 with share-sheet saving and **offline caching**. New, and still behind the web app in depth.
- **Browser extensions for Chrome, Firefox and Edge.** No Safari.
- **Imports from Pocket and browser bookmark HTML.** Directly relevant in 2027, when most migrations into any tool are rescues out of a dead one.

What is absent is a coherent set rather than a list of gaps: **no semantic search, no AI summaries, no AI Q&A, no YouTube tooling, no reader queue, no text-to-speech, no reading progress, no one-click save-all-tabs, and no free hosted tier.**

## Durability and portability: the archivist's own archive

This is the section our 2026 edition did not write, and it is a strange one to write about Linkwarden, because the product is *literally* a durability tool. So the question is whether the tool itself is durable.

The category's recent record makes the question fair rather than paranoid. **Omnivore shut down in November 2024** after its team was acqui-hired by ElevenLabs; users had until **November 30, 2024** to export before deletion. **Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025**; export access and the API were disabled on **November 12, 2025**, data deletion began then, and Premium subscribers received prorated refunds. Both are marked `shutdown` in our data layer. Two well-loved, widely recommended tools, gone inside eight months — one of them backed by a foundation rather than a venture round.

Against that record Linkwarden scores well on every axis we can verify.

**Licence.** AGPL-3.0, with all features included when self-hosted. That is the strongest licence posture in the category: no open-core cripple, no paid gate on the self-hosted build. If Linkwarden the company disappeared tomorrow, a self-hosted install keeps running and the code can be forked — which is precisely what happened to Omnivore's codebase.

**Revenue model.** Funded by the Cloud subscription. That is a real business rather than a free service with no revenue, and the free-service-with-no-revenue model is the one that killed Omnivore. It also means the Cloud product has a reason to keep existing.

**Export path.** This is where Linkwarden is close to unimpeachable. Settings export your links as **standard bookmark HTML or JSON** — formats every other manager can read — archived page copies including screenshots and PDFs can be **downloaded**, and a **documented REST API** allows full programmatic export. Self-hosters already have the database and the file storage. There is no version of leaving Linkwarden where your archive is trapped.

**Import path.** Pocket and browser bookmark HTML are both supported, which makes Linkwarden a viable landing spot for a rescued library rather than only a fresh start. Our [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity) is worth reading before any migration: measured on September 26, 2026, it found **0 of 261 items parsed** from a Pocket export's HTML preview file and **43 of 43** from a Chrome bookmark export. The file you feed an importer matters more than the importer's reputation.

**The one durability risk that is specific to Linkwarden.** Its whole value proposition is *copies* — screenshots, PDFs, readable text, Wayback submissions. That means your archive's size grows with every save, and it grows in binary blobs rather than in text. A self-hosted Linkwarden that has not had its storage watched for two years is a self-hosted Linkwarden that eventually stops archiving. The failure mode here is not the vendor disappearing; it is your disk filling up quietly. Budget for it, and test a restore once.

## Strengths

- **Preservation nobody else matches.** A screenshot, a PDF and a readable copy for every link, automatically, plus Wayback Machine submission. If link rot has ever cost you a citation, this is the cure.
- **Open source done properly.** Clean AGPL-3.0 licensing with genuine self-host parity — no feature held back for the paid cloud. No lock-in anywhere.
- **Real collaboration.** Shared collections with per-member permissions and public collection pages. Rare in this category, well executed, and the cheapest way to get it.
- **Aggressive, honest pricing.** $36/year per user on Cloud, or $0 self-hosted. The money argument is very hard to lose.
- **A 30,000-link allowance that is effectively unlimited**, and expandable via support if it is not.
- **Full-text search into archived content**, not just titles and tags — a bigger haystack than most rivals give you.
- **Local-model AI tagging** for self-hosters who want automation without a cloud in the loop.
- **Export in standard formats plus a documented API.** The best portability story among the hosted-capable tools we cover.
- **A maturing client story.** Official iOS and Android apps with offline caching, three browser extensions, a REST API.

## Weaknesses

- **Retrieval is entirely on you.** Search is keyword and full-text. If you cannot recall words that appear in the page, no number of archived formats will surface it. There is **no semantic search** — the gap we keep measuring across this category in the [AI bookmark manager guide](/blog/best-ai-bookmark-manager-2027).
- **The AI stops at tagging.** No summaries for triaging a backlog, no Q&A over your library, nothing that resurfaces an old save. Compared with [Karakeep](/blog/karakeep-review-2027) — its closest open-source rival — Linkwarden's intelligence layer is a clear step behind.
- **It is not a reading app.** No reader queue, no text-to-speech, no reading progress. You can read an archived copy and highlight it; there is no workflow built around getting through a pile. Heavy readers need something alongside it.
- **No free hosted tier.** The 14-day trial is an evaluation window. Free means running your own server, full stop.
- **Self-hosting has real appetite.** A headless browser doing captures, three copies per save, and storage that grows fast. It wants a proper server, not the smallest VPS.
- **No Safari extension.** Chrome, Firefox and Edge only. Apple-only households are served on mobile and the web app, not in the browser.
- **The mobile apps are still catching up.** Launched late 2025, and our current sheet still records them as behind the web experience.
- **No native desktop apps.** Browser-first, and desktop-app fans should know it.
- **No YouTube tooling and no one-click save-all-tabs.** Increasingly standard elsewhere at similar prices.

## Who should skip Linkwarden

Real disqualifiers, in the order people hit them:

- **Your problem is retrieval, not rot.** If your library's failure mode is "I saved it and never found it again," archiving it in three formats does not fix that. This is the single most common reason people leave, and it is not a bug Linkwarden intends to fix — preservation *is* the product.
- **You want a free hosted account.** There is no permanent free Cloud tier. The 14-day trial expires and then it is $3/month per user billed yearly, or self-host.
- **You live in Safari.** No Safari extension. Chrome, Firefox and Edge only.
- **You want to read in it.** No reader queue, no text-to-speech, no reading progress. Pair it with a read-it-later app rather than expecting it to be one — our [read-it-later roundup for 2027](/blog/best-read-it-later-apps-2027) ranks those.
- **You want AI to summarise a backlog or answer questions.** Tagging is the whole AI surface.
- **You have no always-on machine and no intention of getting one**, *and* $36/year per user feels like too much. That combination leaves you nowhere, and you should look at Raindrop.io's free tier instead.
- **You need SSO, SCIM or directory-based provisioning for a school or a company.** Linkwarden has per-collection permissions, not identity management. Neither does Marqly Teams, for what it is worth — if SSO is a hard requirement, this whole category is the wrong shelf.
- **Your disk is small and your saving is heavy.** Three copies per save plus optional video pulls fills storage faster than a plain bookmark list, and an archive that stops archiving is worse than no archive.

And who should *not* skip it: researchers, journalists, lawyers and citation-heavy writers who need pages as they were; teams and classrooms sharing curated collections with permissions; self-hosters who want a polished, actively developed, properly licensed tool; and anyone burned by a shutdown who has decided that the only library they trust is one they hold. Inside that profile Linkwarden is the best preservation tool available, at the lowest price in the category.

## Verdict

Linkwarden in 2027 is still the best preservation-first bookmark manager in the open-source world, and the case for it has strengthened rather than weakened since we last reviewed it. The mobile apps have had a year in the field. The licence remains AGPL-3.0 with genuine self-host parity, which in a category that lost Omnivore and Pocket inside eight months is not a detail — it is the reason a self-hosted Linkwarden is a permanent library rather than a rented one. Cloud at $36/year per user is the cheapest collaborative archive we know of, and the export path is standard bookmark HTML or JSON with downloadable archives and a documented API on top.

Its limitation is unchanged and structural: the intelligence layer is thin. Keyword and full-text retrieval, tagging as the only AI, no summaries, no Q&A, no reading workflow. Linkwarden keeps things magnificently and finds things literally. If permanence is what you need, keep it as your vault and stop reading reviews. If what you need is to *find* what you saved — to describe a half-remembered page and get it back — that is the other half of the problem, and it is the half [Marqly](https://app.marqly.com) was built around. The free plan needs no card, stores up to 100 bookmarks with keyword search across the whole library, and imports browser bookmark HTML, Raindrop collections or the `list.csv` from inside a Pocket export archive.
