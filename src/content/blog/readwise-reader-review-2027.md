Readwise Reader folds articles, RSS feeds, email newsletters, PDFs, EPUBs, Twitter threads and YouTube transcripts into a single triage inbox, with the best highlight-and-review pipeline in the business attached to it. **It costs $119.88/year on the Full plan billed annually, or $12.99/month paid monthly, and there is no permanent free tier** — only a 30-day trial. It suits people who read at volume and want what they read to stick. It does not suit people whose real problem is a growing pile of saves they cannot find things in, because Reader's search is keyword-based and its AI works on the document in front of you rather than across the library behind you.

## What changed since our 2026 review

Five corrections, and one of them is an error about our own product that we should not have shipped.

1. **We mislabelled our own offline support.** The 2026 comparison table showed offline reading as absent for Marqly. That was wrong, and it is the kind of error we should catch in our own favour rather than only in our competitors'. Offline reading ships on Pro in the **web app and the iOS app**; it is saved **per device**, with no server copy and no cross-device sync, and videos are not cached. It is not on the free plan, and the browser extensions and the Android app do not have it. Reader still wins this row — its offline story covers mobile properly and has done for longer — but the row was mislabelled, and the correction is the reason this section exists.
2. **Reader's browser support is narrower than we implied.** The 2026 edition said "extensions for the major browsers." Our fact sheet lists **Chrome and Firefox**. There is no Edge or Safari extension recorded, which matters if you are on Safari and assumed you were covered.
3. **Reader's tagging is manual, not "limited."** The 2026 table said auto-tagging was "Limited." The fact sheet records automatic AI tagging as **absent** and its cons list says plainly that "tagging is manual rather than automatic." Those are different claims and the second one is the true one.
4. **We dated the launch wrong.** The 2026 edition said Reader "launched publicly in 2023." Our fact sheet records it entering **public beta in 2022**, and it has since left beta to become the Readwise team's flagship.
5. **The student discount we quoted in 2026 is real, and we can now cite it.** Our August 2026 fact sheet did not carry it, so an earlier draft of this review declined to repeat it. We have since verified it directly on readwise.io/pricing (2026-10-29), which states: *"We offer a 50% discount to students and other members of academia."* The mechanism matters and is easy to get wrong — you sign up, email hello@readwise.io to say you are a student **before** subscribing, and then subscribe through the Readwise website. The discount is not applied at checkout and there is no self-serve toggle, so if you subscribe first you have to sort it out afterwards. Applied to the Full plan that takes $119.88/year to about $60/year. The fact sheet has been updated accordingly.

What did not change is the structure of the deal: no free tier, Reader bundled into Full, and the Lite plan without the app.

## What is Readwise Reader?

Reader is the read-it-later app from Readwise, the service that made its name by syncing Kindle and app highlights into a daily spaced-repetition review. It entered public beta in **2022** as, in the team's own framing, a read-it-later app on steroids — and the description still holds. Reader wants to be the single place where everything you read arrives, gets triaged, gets read, gets highlighted, and gets remembered.

It runs on **web, iOS and Android**, with extensions for **Chrome and Firefox**, and offline reading on mobile. Everything you highlight flows into the Readwise review system automatically. The two products are one subscription and one ecosystem, which is simultaneously the best and the most annoying thing about the offer.

## Key features

**One inbox for everything.** The core idea. Web articles, RSS feeds, email newsletters via a personal forwarding address, PDFs, EPUBs, Twitter and X threads, and YouTube videos with transcripts all land in one queue with a shared triage workflow — later, archive, delete. If your reading is currently scattered across six apps, Reader genuinely consolidates it, and consolidation is most of the argument for the price.

**Best-in-class highlighting and review.** Highlight anything, including on the open web through the extension, tag it, annotate it, and it syncs into Readwise's daily review, which resurfaces your highlights on a spaced-repetition schedule. This is the feature the company was built on and no competitor comes close for retention-focused readers. It is also the feature you are paying for whether or not you use it, because it is bundled rather than optional.

**Ghostreader.** Reader's built-in AI summarises documents, answers questions about the text you are reading, defines terms in context and runs custom prompts, including against YouTube transcripts. Genuinely useful, with a specific shape worth understanding — its own section below.

**Keyboard-first triage.** Reader is built for volume. Nearly everything has a shortcut, and power users process hundreds of items a week without touching a mouse. Our fact sheet records the keyboard-first design as the fastest in the category. The learning curve is real and so is the payoff.

**Text-to-speech.** Competent TTS on mobile for listening on the move. Not the headline feature, but it retires a separate audiobook-ish app for some people.

**Highlight export into your notes app.** Because Reader rides on Readwise's sync engine, every highlight can flow onward into Notion, Obsidian, Roam, Evernote and other note systems automatically. For people building a permanent knowledge base, this pipeline — read in Reader, highlight, wake up with the highlight already filed in your vault — is quietly the strongest reason to pay. No other reading app has integrations this deep.

**A documented public API.** Programmatic access to your library, which our 2026 edition did not mention. Combined with the export story, it is the reason Reader's lock-in risk is low despite the price.

## What Reader does well

- **Consolidation.** RSS reader, newsletter inbox, read-it-later queue, PDF and EPUB reader, and YouTube transcript viewer, replaced by one app. For many subscribers that retires two or three other paid tools, which reframes $119.88 considerably.
- **The highlight-to-memory pipeline.** Nothing else turns reading into retained knowledge this systematically. If retention is your goal rather than retrieval, this is the product.
- **Cross-platform with offline included.** Web, iOS, Android, Chrome, Firefox, and offline reading on mobile — which Raindrop.io and mymind both lack entirely.
- **Every content type in one place.** Articles, feeds, newsletters, PDFs, EPUBs, YouTube, Twitter threads. The breadth is unmatched.
- **YouTube transcripts you can read and highlight like an article.** Synced transcripts, queryable, which is a real differentiator rather than a checkbox.
- **Development velocity.** The team ships constantly and publishes changelogs that read like a proud engineering blog. This product is visibly alive, which is not a small thing in a category that lost two of its most-recommended tools in eight months.
- **Honest bundling — for the right person.** If you were going to pay for Readwise anyway, Reader is effectively the best reading app you will ever get for free.

## Where Reader falls short

- **The price.** $119.88/year is two to four times the category. Worth it for its target user; that user reads professionally or close to it.
- **No free tier.** After 30 days you pay or you leave. Casual users never get to grow into the product, and growth is exactly what an inbox paradigm requires.
- **Complexity.** The inbox metaphor, the shortcut vocabulary, the settings depth — Reader asks for onboarding effort the way pro tools do. People who want "save article, find article" will feel they bought a cockpit.
- **Retrieval is document-first, not memory-first.** Search is solid full-text with filters, and Ghostreader interrogates the document you are in. But searching your whole library by *meaning* is not the workflow Reader is built around, and our fact sheet records semantic search as absent.
- **Tagging is manual.** No automatic tagging on any plan. In a product designed for volume, that is an odd omission.
- **No public sharing of collections.** You cannot publish a reading list as a page someone else can open without an account.
- **No save-as-PDF.** Reader reads PDFs natively; it does not produce them.
- **The bundle cuts both ways.** No standalone Reader plan means no cheap path for people who want the reading app without the review system, and the Lite plan is not that path.
- **It can become a second job.** An inbox metaphor means inbox pressure. Some readers find that a relaxing habit turned into a triage obligation. That is a temperament observation rather than a flaw, but know yourself before you pour six content firehoses into one queue.

## The Lite plan trap: Reader is Full-plan only

This is the single most common pricing mistake people make with Readwise, so it gets its own section.

Readwise sells two plans. The cheaper one is **Lite at $5.59/month billed annually**, and it looks like a bargain — roughly $67/year against $119.88, which is a 44% saving. It is also, for most people reading this review, completely useless, because **Lite does not include the Reader app.** Lite covers the classic Readwise service: you connect Kindle, Apple Books, Pocket-era highlights or a browser extension, and Readwise emails you a daily review of highlights you already made elsewhere.

So the decision tree is short:

- **You want the reading app** — the inbox, the feeds, the newsletters, the PDFs and EPUBs, Ghostreader — **then you are on Full at $119.88/year.** There is no cheaper configuration.
- **You already read everywhere else and only want spaced repetition over your highlights**, then Lite at $67.08/year is the right buy and this review is about a product you do not need.
- **You want both but only one at a time**, there is no split. Full includes Lite's functionality; Lite never includes Full's.

The practical consequence is that Reader has no entry price below $119.88/year. Compare that with a market where Raindrop Pro is $28/year, Marqly Pro is $72/year with a standing $49 first-year offer, and Instapaper Premium is $59.99/year. Reader is not expensive for what it does. It is expensive to *start*.

## No free tier: what 30 days can and cannot tell you

Reader's trial is 30 days with full access, which is generous by duration and inadequate by structure, for one reason: **an inbox product takes longer than a month to prove itself.**

What 30 days does show you: whether the keyboard-first triage feels fast or hostile, whether Ghostreader is useful on your actual reading, whether the highlight-to-Notion or highlight-to-Obsidian pipeline works with your setup, and whether consolidating your feeds into one queue feels like relief or like pressure. Those are real questions and the trial answers them well.

What 30 days cannot show you: whether the spaced-repetition review changes what you remember, because retention effects appear over months; whether the library stays navigable at two or three thousand items, because you will not have accumulated them; and whether the subscription still feels worth it in year two, when the novelty of the shortcut vocabulary has worn off and the inbox is just work.

There is also no free tier to fall back to. In a category where Raindrop.io gives unlimited bookmarks and collections forever and Marqly's free plan holds up to 100 bookmarks with keyword search across the whole library, Reader is the only major tool with nothing permanent at $0. For a casual saver that is disqualifying on its own, and no trial softens it.

## Ghostreader in 2027: what the AI reaches

Ghostreader is good, and its limits are structural rather than a matter of quality.

**What it does.** It summarises the document you have open. It defines terms in context, which is underrated for dense technical reading. It answers questions about the text in front of you. It runs custom prompts you define. It works against synced YouTube transcripts as well as articles, PDFs and EPUBs. Our fact sheet records AI summaries, chat with saved content, and YouTube AI all as present.

**What it does not do.** It is not a search engine over your library. It reads the document, not the archive. So the query that actually torments people with large collections — "what was that piece I saved in March about the failure of open-plan offices?" — is not a Ghostreader query. It is a search query, and Reader's search is **full-text with filters, keyword-based, with semantic search recorded as absent.**

That distinction is the whole difference between a reading tool and a retrieval tool, and it is the reason our [AI bookmark manager guide](/blog/best-ai-bookmark-manager-2027) sorts the market on the query layer rather than the feature list. Reader wins the reading half of that split decisively. It does not compete in the other half.

Worth adding: **automatic tagging is absent too.** In a product built for volume, where the entire pitch is pouring six firehoses into one queue, manual tagging is a genuine bottleneck — and it is the bottleneck that decides whether a 3,000-item Reader library is navigable in year two.

## Portability: the strongest thing Readwise does

After Pocket shut down on **July 8, 2025** and Omnivore shut down in **November 2024**, portability stopped being a nice-to-have and became a scoring criterion. On that criterion Reader is one of the best products in this entire market, and it deserves credit for it.

- **Highlights and notes export continuously** to Notion, Obsidian, Evernote, Roam and Markdown files. Not on request — on a schedule, automatically, in open formats.
- **Your document list exports as CSV.**
- **A documented public API** provides programmatic access to everything, so anything the UI cannot export, a script can.
- **If you cancel, you keep the pipeline's output.** The highlights already sitting in your Obsidian vault are Markdown files on your disk. Cancelling Reader does not retroactively delete your notes.

That is a materially stronger exit than mymind offers (a one-way `cards.csv` from desktop Chrome or Edge, with no re-import format) and stronger than most proprietary tools manage. It does not eliminate the risk of the product disappearing — nothing does — but it means the *work* survives, which is the part that actually hurts to lose.

On the company side: Readwise is bootstrapped and profitable on subscriptions, which makes a Pocket-style portfolio cut or an Omnivore-style acqui-hire less likely. The trade-off is honest and worth naming — the same subscription-funded model is precisely why there is no free plan subsidising casual users.

## Reader in 2027: survivor of the read-it-later die-off

The context around this review has changed more than the product has.

**Omnivore shut down in November 2024** after its team was acqui-hired by ElevenLabs. Users had until November 30, 2024 to export. It was completely free with no paid plan at any point, which is exactly what made the shutdown possible. **Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025**, disabled export access and the Pocket API on **November 12, 2025**, and says remaining data was queued for permanent deletion from that date; Premium subscribers received prorated refunds. Source: [Mozilla's Pocket shutdown notice](https://support.mozilla.org/en-US/kb/future-of-pocket).

Eight months apart, two of the most-recommended reading tools in the world disappeared. Reader survived both, for a reason that is unromantic but real: it charges enough to be a business. In 2027 that changes how the price should be read. **$119.88/year is not only a feature price, it is a durability price.** Free-with-no-revenue is the most fragile structure in software, and the readers who lost libraries learned that the hard way.

It also changes who Reader's competition is. In 2024 you compared Reader against Pocket and Omnivore, both free. In 2027 you compare it against paid survivors and against free tools with actual business models — Raindrop.io at $28/year for Pro and unlimited free storage, Instapaper Premium at $5.99/month or $59.99/year, and Wallabag free and open source under MIT when self-hosted or €11/year on official hosting. Against that field Reader is the most expensive and the most capable, and both of those are true at once.

One pricing note that catches people arriving from older articles: **Instapaper Premium is $5.99/month or $59.99/year, and it roughly doubled in late 2023 from $3/month and $30/year.** Plenty of comparison pages still quote the old figure. It remains the cheapest dedicated reading queue in our data, but it is no longer the $30-a-year afterthought those pages describe, and the annual gap between it and Reader is now $59.89 rather than the $89.88 they imply.

One import note for anyone arriving with an old archive: Reader imports a Pocket export, but it does not import browser bookmark files. Marqly reads the **`list.csv` from inside a Pocket export archive** — not the preview file in that archive, whose list markup is not the Netscape bookmark format and does not parse. Marqly also takes browser bookmarks and Raindrop's HTML export. We measured what actually survives a migration in the [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity), and our [Pocket alternatives guide for 2027](/blog/best-pocket-alternatives-2027) ranks the landing spots, including the case where you never exported at all.

Real disqualifiers, in the order people hit them:

- **You save links and lose them.** That is retrieval, and Reader is built for reading. Semantic search is absent and tagging is manual, so a 3,000-item Reader library is only as navigable as your own discipline makes it.

- **You want to publish a reading list to other people.** No public sharing of collections. Compare a tool with public boards — ours are free and view-only with no signup needed, which is the reason teachers use them.
- **You want the app to file things for you.** No automatic tagging on any plan.
- **An inbox stresses you out.** Reader is an inbox by design. If triage pressure turns reading into work for you, the product will make your life worse at $10 a month.

And who should *not* skip it: professional and heavy readers processing dozens of items daily; highlight-driven learners who want spaced repetition to make reading stick; newsletter and RSS power users drowning in subscriptions across apps; existing Readwise subscribers, who already own Reader and should go use it; and Obsidian, Notion and Roam users who want highlights landing in their vault without lifting a finger — for that last group the export pipeline alone can justify the subscription.

## Verdict

Readwise Reader is the most powerful reading tool on the market and the most expensive one we cover. In 2027 the price is $119.88/year on the Full plan billed annually, or $155.88/year paid monthly, with no permanent free tier and no way to buy the reading app without also buying the review system — the cheaper Lite plan at $67.08/year does not include it. Its limits are consistent rather than accidental: keyword search, manual tagging, document-scoped AI, no public sharing, Chrome and Firefox only.

Its strengths are equally consistent. Nothing else consolidates articles, feeds, newsletters, PDFs, EPUBs and YouTube transcripts into one triage inbox and then turns your highlights into retained knowledge on a schedule, with continuous export into your note vault and a documented API behind it. Its portability is the best in the proprietary half of this market, and its bootstrapped, subscription-funded business model is the reason it outlived two free competitors.
