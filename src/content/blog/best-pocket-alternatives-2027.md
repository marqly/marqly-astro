## What happened to Pocket, exactly

Mozilla announced Pocket's shutdown in May 2025 and turned off the service on July 8, 2025. The timeline that matters in 2027:

1. **July 8, 2025 — shutdown.** Mozilla ended the Pocket service and turned off new saves. Apps and extensions stopped syncing.
2. **November 12, 2025 — export window closed.** Export access and the API were disabled, and Mozilla says remaining data was queued for permanent deletion from that date.
3. **Premium subscribers** received prorated refunds for remaining subscription time.
4. **2027 — the archive-rescue phase.** If you exported, your library is a ZIP on a disk somewhere and the tools below will read the CSV inside it. If you did not, there is no supported recovery path.

Source: [Mozilla's Pocket shutdown notice](https://support.mozilla.org/en-US/kb/future-of-pocket), verified October 6, 2026.

Worth remembering what you are replacing, because it explains the shape of this market. Pocket's search was **title-and-URL only unless you paid for Premium full-text search**. Tagging was **fully manual**, with suggested tags a Premium extra. Highlights were **capped at three per article on the free plan**, with no notes or annotations. Pocket was pleasant to save into and close to useless to retrieve from — which is why "I have 4,000 saves and cannot find any of them" was the single most common complaint, and why semantic search is now the main axis this list is sorted on.

## What to do in 2027 if you never exported

This is the part most Pocket-alternative articles skip, and in 2027 it is half the audience. Check these four places in order before you accept the loss:

1. **Your downloads folder and cloud drives.** Look for a ZIP with `ril_export` in the name, or a folder containing `list.csv`. Pocket's export produced an archive, and most people downloaded it to a default location and forgot. Search your file system for `list.csv` and for `.zip` files created around the middle of 2025.
2. **Any tool you already synced Pocket to.** Readwise, IFTTT-style automations, Notion databases and some RSS bridges pulled Pocket saves on a schedule. If you ever connected Pocket to anything, that thing may hold a partial copy.
3. **Your browser's own bookmark file.** If you ever ran a Pocket-to-browser export, Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari each hold a standard HTML bookmark file you can export today — and that file imports into every tool below.
4. **Your email.** Search for Pocket or Mozilla messages from mid-2025. Shutdown notices sometimes contained a direct export link, and while those links are dead, the email may confirm whether you completed a download and when.

If all four come up empty: Mozilla states that export access was disabled on November 12, 2025 and that data deletion began from that date, so there is no supported recovery. The practical move is to start fresh with a tool whose export path you have tested on day one — see the durability column below — and to treat the loss as the argument for that habit rather than as bad luck.

## Two shutdowns in eight months: how to pick a tool that survives

**Omnivore shut down in November 2024** after its team was acqui-hired by ElevenLabs. Users had until **November 30, 2024** to export before deletion. Omnivore was completely free, with no paid plan at any point — and that free-with-no-revenue model is precisely what made the shutdown possible. The open-source code remains on GitHub and community forks offer self-hosted deployments, but the forks are volunteer-maintained with no guarantees, and self-hosting the original stack is genuinely hard (PostgreSQL, Elasticsearch and multiple microservices).

**Pocket shut down on July 8, 2025.** Eight months later. Different owner, different reason — a portfolio cut rather than a failed business — and the same outcome for anyone who had not exported.

The lesson for choosing in 2027 is not "avoid free tools." It is that three properties determine whether your library outlives the vendor, and you can check all three in ten minutes:

- **Can you get everything out, in a documented format, without asking anyone?** Wallabag, Obsidian and the self-hosted pair are strongest here by construction. Marqly exports CSV carrying URL, title, description and tags, with a complete account copy including highlights, notes and boards available from support. Raindrop exports cleanly.
- **Is there a revenue model that does not depend on you being the product or on a single acquirer's mood?** Paid plans with real subscribers (Raindrop, Readwise, Marqly) and long-lived open-source projects with a hosted option funding development (Wallabag since 2013) score better than free-with-no-revenue.
- **Is the code open source, so a shutdown leaves a fork rather than a hole?** Wallabag (MIT), Linkwarden (AGPL-3.0) and Karakeep all survive their company. Proprietary tools do not.

## 2. Raindrop.io — best free, general-purpose replacement

Raindrop is the most common straight-across Pocket replacement and the easiest to recommend on price alone: **unlimited bookmarks, unlimited collections, tags and every app** — web, Mac, Windows, Linux, iOS, Android, and extensions for Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari — free, with no expiry and up to three highlights per bookmark. Public collections share a curated list from a link. It saves articles, videos, PDFs, images and whole pages, and it is a mature, stable product.

Where it stops short of the 2027 frontier is retrieval. Search on free is titles and tags; **full-text search requires Pro**, and even then it is keyword-shaped rather than semantic. If your Pocket complaint was "I can never find anything I saved," Raindrop will not fix that — it will give you a much nicer place to lose things. Pro at $28/year or $3.25/month adds full-text search, Stella AI save-time tag and collection suggestions, permanent page copies, annotations and daily backups, and permanent page copies are the strongest link-rot defence among the hosted options.

**Who it's best for:** people who want a free, dependable Pocket-shaped tool and do not care about AI. **Skip it if** retrieval is your actual pain, because no price tier changes the keyword-only model.

- **Pros:** the most generous free tier here; apps on every platform including Linux; polished visual organisation with nested collections; Pro is cheap and adds permanent page copies.
- **Cons:** no semantic search at any price; full-text search is paywalled; organisation is entirely manual.
- **Price:** free (unlimited) · Pro $28/year or $3.25/month.

## 3. Wallabag — best if the reason you are here is that a vendor vanished

Wallabag is open source under **MIT**, free to self-host, and has been maintained continuously since it started in France in 2013. That combination is the direct answer to the Pocket and Omnivore lesson: there is no company that can decide to shut it down, and if the project ever stalls, a working copy is already on your server. It is a read-it-later tool first, which means it reproduces the behaviour Pocket refugees actually miss — save a page, read a clean distraction-free copy later.

If you do not want to run a server, official **wallabag.it** hosting is **€11/year** with all features included, plus **€4 for 3 months** and a **€30/year supporter plan** that funds development. That is the cheapest paid option in this article and, unlike a free tier, it has no cap.

The trade is that Wallabag has no AI of any kind: no auto-tagging, no summaries, no semantic search. Organisation and retrieval are on you, and it is weaker as a general bookmark organiser than as a reading queue.

**Who it's best for:** anyone whose primary 2027 criterion is "this will still exist," plus technical users happy to self-host. **Skip it if** you want AI retrieval or a polished native app experience.

- **Pros:** MIT open source, free to self-host, 13-year continuous maintenance history; clean read-it-later rendering; hosted option at €11/year with everything included; no proprietary format.
- **Cons:** no AI features at all; self-hosting is real setup and maintenance; smaller app ecosystem; reading-focused rather than organiser-focused.
- **Price:** free self-hosted · €11/year hosted · €4 for 3 months · €30/year supporter.

## 4. Karakeep — best self-hosted option that actually has AI

Karakeep (formerly Hoarder, renamed in 2025) is the answer if you want both properties at once: your own server *and* automatic organisation. Self-hosting is fully free and open source with all features included, and an LLM you configure — an OpenAI-compatible key or a local Ollama model — **auto-tags everything**, with full-text and semantic search across it all. Extensions for Chrome, Firefox and Safari plus iOS and Android apps make it unusually complete for a self-hosted project, and local-LLM support means the tagging can be private as well as free.

For a Pocket rescue, the caveat is that Karakeep's import path is a standard bookmark import rather than a Pocket-specific one, so plan on converting your `list.csv` to a standard format first. The hosted Karakeep Cloud is in public beta with a **$0 tier capped at 10 bookmarks** and a **$4/month** Pro tier — ten bookmarks is a preview, so "free Karakeep" means your own server.

**Who it's best for:** technical users who want AI organisation without handing a vendor their library. **Skip it if** you do not want to administer software, or if AI costs (API fees or local GPU) are a problem.

- **Pros:** AI tagging and semantic search, free, under your own roof; local-LLM support for private AI; full apps and extensions; a cheap $4/month cloud Pro as an exit from self-hosting.
- **Cons:** you run the server, updates and backups; AI costs surface eventually; the cloud free tier caps at 10 bookmarks; Pocket import needs a conversion step.
- **Price:** free self-hosted · Cloud Pro $4/month (annual price not published).

## 5. Linkwarden — best for archiving so link rot cannot win

Linkwarden is open source under **AGPL-3.0 with all features included when self-hosted**, and it is built around **full-page preservation**: every bookmark gets an archived copy. For a library that has already survived one vendor's disappearance, that is a different kind of insurance — it protects against the *pages* dying, not just the service. A 2019 Pocket archive is full of dead URLs by 2027, and Linkwarden is the only tool here that would have kept the content.

It is collaborative, supports highlights and RSS, has real mobile apps and browser extensions, and offers optional AI tagging if you wire up a provider. The cloud plan is **$3/month per user billed yearly (25% off) or $4/month billed monthly**, supports up to 30,000 links per user, and has no free tier beyond a trial — so free Linkwarden means self-hosted Linkwarden.

**Who it's best for:** preservationists, researchers, and anyone whose old archive is already full of dead links. **Skip it if** AI retrieval is the priority (Karakeep is stronger there) or you do not want to run a server.

- **Pros:** free forever self-hosted with every feature; full-page archives of everything — the best link-rot answer here; active development with mobile apps and extensions; collaboration built in.
- **Cons:** "free" costs a server and your maintenance time; AI is an optional bolt-on; cloud has no free tier beyond a trial; setup is real work.
- **Price:** free self-hosted · Cloud $3/user/month billed yearly or $4/month billed monthly.

**Who it's best for:** commuters and multitaskers whose queue is mostly long reads they would rather hear. **Skip it if** you need deep search, a big organised archive, or Android.

## How to actually move your Pocket archive

1. **Find the archive.** Locate the ZIP Pocket produced. Inside you should see a CSV (commonly `list.csv`) and an HTML preview file. If you only have the HTML file, see step 2.
2. **Use the CSV, not the HTML.** The HTML preview is a `<ul><li>` layout, not the Netscape bookmark format, and importers that expect Netscape will not parse it — we measured **0 of 261 items** from a genuine export. If the CSV is missing, convert what you have with the free [Pocket Export Converter](/tools/pocket-export-converter) and inspect the result in the [Bookmark File Viewer](/tools/bookmark-file-viewer).
3. **Clean before you import.** A multi-year Pocket library is full of duplicates and dead links. Run it through the free [Duplicate Bookmark Finder](/tools/duplicate-bookmark-finder) and [Dead Link Checker](/tools/dead-link-checker) first; importing 4,000 links of which 900 are dead just relocates the problem.
4. **Import, then verify.** Marqly's importer takes `.html/.htm/.csv` files up to 10 MB on free and 30 MB on Pro, with 10,000 bookmarks per file. Titles, URLs, descriptions and tags carry across; folder structure flattens to at most two board levels; **original save dates do not** — imported items take the import date.
5. **Tag once, or let Pro do it.** Auto-tagging an import is a Pro feature. On free, your existing Pocket tags come across and nothing new is added, so budget an evening if you want the archive organised by hand.
6. **Test the exit on day one.** Export something back out immediately and confirm the file is readable. That single habit is the actual lesson of Pocket and Omnivore.

Follow the [Pocket migration guide](/migrate/pocket) for the tool-specific steps, or start from the universal [Migration Center](/migrate). All of the cleaning tools above are in the [free tools directory](/tools).

## What actually matters in 2027

**Retrieval beats capture.** Pocket proved that a delightful save button plus title-only search produces a library nobody uses. If you take one thing from this list, take that the differentiating feature in 2027 is finding things, and the only capability that meaningfully changes finding things is semantic search — Pro in Marqly, absent from Raindrop and Instapaper at any price, and available self-hosted in Karakeep.

**Durability is now a feature you can evaluate.** Two major tools died in eight months. Check the export path, the revenue model and whether the code is open source before you commit four thousand links to anyone. Wallabag, Linkwarden and Karakeep are strongest on that axis by construction; every hosted tool in this table is weaker, including ours.

**Link rot is the slow failure.** An archive rescued in 2027 is full of pages that died between 2019 and now. Full-page archives (Linkwarden), permanent copies (Raindrop Pro, Instapaper Premium) and local save-as-PDF (Marqly, processed in Chrome and Edge) are the three real defences; most tools offer none.
