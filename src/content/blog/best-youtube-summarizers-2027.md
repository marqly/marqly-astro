**The best YouTube summarizer for most people in 2027 is Marqly**, because the summary lives where you watch — an AI card on the watch page with a streaming TL;DR, key sections, a playback-synced transcript and a chat tab that answers questions from the video — and because saving the video attaches its transcript to a library you can search by meaning. **Glasp is the best free option on the watch page.** **NoteGPT is the pick for students who want mind maps and flashcards.** **Recall handles videos measured in hours.** **Readwise Reader, Matter and LINER are verified additions to this edition**, and **ChatGPT remains the free manual route with a real ceiling.**

The difference between these tools is not summary quality — everyone calls a capable model now, and the text is fine everywhere. It is **where the summary lives** (on the watch page, or in a separate app you have to visit), whether you get the **transcript**, whether you can **chat with the video**, and **what survives afterwards**.

## The four things that actually separate these tools

1. **Where the summary lives.** On the watch page, at zero friction, or in a separate workspace you have to visit while the video plays. This decides whether you actually use it.
2. **The transcript.** A summary is a receipt; the transcript is the asset. Playback-synced, copyable and — critically — retained with the save.
3. **Chat.** Asking the video a question beats reading a paragraph about it, especially for lectures and long interviews.
4. **What survives.** Whether the summary and transcript land somewhere searchable, or evaporate when you close the tab. Three of the nine tools below keep nothing.

## 2. Glasp — best free watch-page summaries

Glasp makes the most widely used YouTube summary extension in the category, and its free allowance is the most generous per day here: **three YouTube summaries a day**, alongside 30 PDF uploads and 20 PDF chat messages a month, plus unlimited highlighting. Summaries and transcripts appear on the watch page, and your notes flow into a public Glasp profile with a community feed.

Paid plans are where the video features get serious: **advanced YouTube summaries, channel tracking, PDF chat, audio transcription, and an AI clone that answers questions from your highlights**. **Pro is $12.50/month billed yearly ($150/year) or $15/month billed monthly**, and the Unlimited tier is **$30/month billed yearly ($360/year)** — after prices rose about 20–25% in May 2026. Subscriptions are explicitly non-refundable, and Glasp publishes a **40% discount on Pro for students and educators**, billed yearly. Platform reach is the broadest in the niche — Chrome, Safari, Edge and Firefox plus iOS and Android apps rebuilt in 2026, including a mobile Safari extension.

Two honest caveats. Free highlights are **public by default**, and privacy costs Pro. And we printed a paid quota figure for Glasp in 2026 that our fact sheet does not support, so this edition names no advanced-summary allowance — check the pricing page for the current number. There is also no full-text search, no offline mode and no full-page archiving, and Glasp is run by its two co-founders, which is a real longevity consideration for a tool you would build a study habit on.

**Who it's best for:** people who summarise a handful of videos a day for free and are comfortable highlighting in public, or who already use Glasp for reading. **Skip it if** your highlights must stay private without paying, or you want a searchable transcript library — its search is not full-text and not semantic.

- **Pros:** three free summaries a day, the most generous daily allowance here; summary and transcript on the watch page; channel tracking and audio transcription on paid; the strongest export pipeline in the niche — Markdown, CSV, HTML, JSON plus Notion, Obsidian and Readwise; broadest platform coverage; student discount on Pro yearly.

## 3. NoteGPT — best for students and study materials

NoteGPT is a learning workspace: paste a YouTube link — it handles other platforms too — and get a summary, transcript, chat, **mind maps and flashcards**. The trade-off is friction: everything happens in NoteGPT's own workspace rather than on YouTube, so you are tab-switching while you watch. The quota system also takes reading. Our 2026 edition recorded a small free monthly allowance, **Pro at $9/month for 1,000 basic quotas**, and an **Unlimited plan at $19.92/month billed annually ($239/year)**, with heavy users ending up on the annual plan. **Those figures were not re-verified for this edition** — we maintain no dated fact sheet for NoteGPT — so confirm them on the vendor's pricing page before subscribing.

**Who it's best for:** students who need flashcards and mind maps from lectures and will work in a separate workspace to get them. **Skip it if** you want zero friction on the watch page, or you want the video to end up in a searchable library alongside everything else you save.

- **Pros:** mind maps, flashcards and notes — the best study-output pipeline here; handles more than YouTube, including PDFs and audio; chat with the video content; Pro pricing is reasonable for what it bundles.
- **Cons:** lives in a separate workspace, so constant tab-switching; the quota and credit system is confusing; heavy users end up on the annual plan; more tool than you need for a quick TL;DR; no dated fact sheet, so pricing is carried forward and unverified.

**Who it's best for:** people who summarise marathon content and want a linked knowledge base of the results. **Skip it if** you want to ask the video questions, or you want summaries without leaving YouTube.

## 5. Readwise Reader — best if you highlight and query what you watch

Readwise Reader is the addition that most changes this list, because it treats a YouTube video as a document. Videos get **synced transcripts you can read, highlight and query exactly like an article**, and the Ghostreader assistant answers questions against the text. Everything you mark flows into the Readwise loop: highlights resurface on a spaced-repetition schedule and export continuously to Notion, Obsidian, Evernote and Roam, with a documented public API.

If your reason for summarising a video is to retain and reuse it — a lecture series, a research interview, a conference talk you will cite — nothing else here closes that loop. It is also the only tool in this roundup where a YouTube transcript becomes a first-class highlightable document with an export path.

The costs are price and location. Reader ships **only with the Readwise Full plan**, and there is **no permanent free tier**: Readwise offers a 30-day trial, after which a subscription is required. Full is $9.99/month billed annually ($119.88/year) or $12.99/month monthly, while the cheaper Lite plan at $5.59/month billed annually excludes Reader entirely. Verified students and educators get 50% off. And the transcript lives in the Reader app, not on the watch page, so you are reading video rather than watching it.

**Who it's best for:** students, researchers and writers who treat video as source material to highlight, cite and review. **Skip it if** you want free, you want the summary while the video plays, or you only need a quick gist.

- **Pros:** synced, highlightable YouTube transcripts treated as documents; query the transcript via Ghostreader; spaced-repetition review that fights forgetting; continuous export to Notion, Obsidian, Evernote and Roam plus a documented API; 50% student and educator discount.
- **Cons:** no permanent free tier; Reader requires the Full plan at $119.88/year; transcripts live in the app rather than on the watch page; keyword rather than semantic search; manual tagging; Reader's extensions are Chrome and Firefox only.

## 6. Matter — best for listening to what a video said

Matter is the audio-first answer and a verified addition to this edition. It **transcribes YouTube videos and podcasts with time-synced text you can highlight**, then reads any of it back with the best text-to-speech in the category — human-like voices with word-by-word tracking, so you can switch between reading and listening mid-transcript. For a two-hour podcast you would rather walk than watch, nothing else here competes.

The free tier is real: **unlimited library, excellent parsing, unlimited tags**. Premium adds HD voices, unlimited highlighting and notes, offline full-text search, and highlight syncing out to Notion, Obsidian and Readwise. Check where you buy: the same annual plan is **$8/month or $60/year on the web** versus **$7.99/month or $79.99/year through the iOS App Store**, $20 more per year for identical features, and a Matter Pro tier at $12.99/month also appears in the App Store.

The limits are structural rather than financial. Matter has **no AI summaries, no chat and no automatic tagging** — it transcribes and reads beautifully, but it will not answer a question about the video. There is **no Android app**; Matter covers iPhone, iPad, Mac, the web and a Chrome extension. There is no public API and no public sharing.

**Who it's best for:** Apple-ecosystem commuters who want to listen to long video and podcast content and highlight what they hear. **Skip it if** you use Android, you want to chat with the video, or you want automatic summaries rather than a transcript you read yourself.

- **Pros:** transcribes YouTube and podcasts with time-synced text; the best text-to-speech in the category; you can highlight the transcript; solid free tier with an unlimited library; highlights sync to Notion, Obsidian and Readwise; newsletter delivery by Gmail or email address.

## 9. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini — the free manual method, and its ceiling

The DIY route: get the video's transcript, paste it into a chat model, and prompt "summarise this, then answer my questions." It works, it is flexible, and free tiers handle short videos. You get total control over the prompt — any format, any language, any depth — and chat is the whole product, so follow-up questions are natural. If you summarise one video a month and enjoy prompting, you do not need to pay anyone.

The friction is real and compounds. You fetch the transcript yourself every time, which is a separate skill — our guide to [getting a YouTube video transcript](/blog/how-to-get-youtube-video-transcript) covers it. Very long videos exceed context limits and need chunking, which breaks cross-referencing between the parts. Timestamps get lost, so a claim in the summary cannot be traced back to the moment in the video. And **nothing persists**: no library, no search, no history tied to the video. It is a workflow you rebuild from scratch every single time.

The paid tiers — ChatGPT Plus at $20/month and its equivalents — raise context limits but do not change the shape of the problem. We maintain no dated fact sheet for any consumer chat model, so treat that price as indicative.

**Who it's best for:** occasional summarisers who like prompting and do not need anything to survive the session. **Skip it if** you summarise weekly, you watch long content, or you want the result findable in six months.

- **Pros:** free, or the subscription you may already pay for; total control over prompt, format, language and depth; follow-up questions are natural; works for any video you can get a transcript for.
- **Cons:** manual transcript-fetching every time; long videos need chunking; timestamps are lost; nothing persists; no watch-page integration; a workflow rebuilt from scratch per video.
- **Price:** free · paid tiers from about $20/month, not re-verified for this edition.

Free tiers sound interchangeable until you price them against your actual volume. Per day or per week:

If you summarise one video a day, the honest answer is Glasp on free or a paid plan somewhere. If you summarise three long lectures a month and want to cite them next year, the free tiers are the wrong axis entirely — retention is, which points to Readwise Reader or Marqly.

8. **You want video moments inside citation-backed research search** → **LINER**, accepting that highlights cannot be exported.
9. **You summarise one video a month and enjoy prompting** → **ChatGPT**, Claude or Gemini.

One filter worth applying to all nine: ask what happens to the summary *afterwards*. If the answer is "it evaporates," you will be re-summarising the same videos next year, and the tool you chose was a rental rather than a library.

## What actually matters in 2027

**Summary quality stopped being the differentiator.** Every tool here calls a capable model and produces acceptable prose. The differences are friction, transcript handling and retention — and retention is the one nobody shops for.

**The transcript is the asset; the summary is the receipt.** A summary you cannot trace back is a rumour about a video. Tools that keep a synced transcript with the save — Marqly, Readwise Reader, Matter — let you verify, quote and search what was actually said. Tools that hand you a paragraph and keep nothing do not. If you will want the video again, buy for the transcript.

**Free tiers are rationed on the axis that matters least.** Most ration the number of summaries, which is the cheap part. The expensive part — keeping the transcript, searching it by meaning, citing it next year — is what the paid tiers actually charge for. Price your volume first, then decide whether retention is worth more than the allowance.
