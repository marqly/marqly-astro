**The best read-it-later app in 2027 is Marqly for most people**, because it is the only tool here whose retrieval works by meaning rather than by remembering words — and retrieval, not saving, is the part this category has never solved. **Readwise Reader is the best pick if you highlight and study what you read.** **Raindrop.io is the best free all-rounder.** **Wallabag is the best pick if the reason you are shopping again is that your last app disappeared.**

A read-it-later app should do two things well: make saving frictionless, and make *coming back* worthwhile. Almost everything on the market nailed the first half by 2015 and has competed on typography ever since. The second half is where libraries go to die, and it is the axis this list is sorted on.

The context has also changed shape. When we last published this ranking, Pocket had been gone about eight months and the question was *what do I switch to.* In 2027 Pocket has been dead for a year and a half, its export window closed on November 12, 2025, and Omnivore has been gone for two years. The category has settled into a small number of business models, and you can now judge a read-it-later app on whether it will still exist when you next need it. That is a new column in our table, and it changed two rankings.

## What changed in read-it-later since our 2026 edition

We re-verified every price and plan limit against its fact sheet rather than carrying 2026 copy forward. Several things we printed last time were wrong, and one entry was not a tool at all.

- **Readwise Reader's price was stale.** We quoted "$12 per month." The Full plan is **$9.99/month billed annually ($119.88/year)** or **$12.99/month billed monthly** — we had printed the monthly rate as the headline price.
- **Instapaper's price was badly stale.** Our 2026 table said "$3/mo." Premium is **$5.99/month or $59.99/year**, roughly doubled in late 2023 from $3/month and $30/year. We also omitted that **free accounts cannot full-text search their own saves** and cap notes at five per month.
- **mymind was described as having no free tier at about $8/month.** Both halves were wrong: a free guest plan caps at **100 cards**, and paid runs **$4.99/month** (The Bookmarker, no AI), **$7.99/month or $79/year** (Student of Life, where AI tagging starts) and **$12.99/month or $129/year** (Mastermind). For this query the decisive detail is that **reading mode is a Mastermind feature**.
- **Raindrop.io was marked as supporting offline reading.** It does not. Pages are cached and Pro keeps permanent copies, but its fact sheet records no offline mode.
- **Matter's free tier was understated and its App Store markup missed.** The free tier is real — unlimited library, strong parsing, unlimited tags — and the same annual plan costs $60 on the web but $79.99 through iOS.
- **Entry eight in 2026 was "Pocket Casts-style hybrids / Omnivore forks."** That was a shrug, not a tool. Omnivore is `status: shutdown` and its forks are volunteer-maintained. **GoodLinks** and **Karakeep** replace it — both verifiable, both absent from the 2026 list.

Two ranking changes follow. **Wallabag moves up from seventh to fifth**, because durability stopped being a niche preference and became a criterion with two data points. **mymind moves down from fifth to ninth**, because a visual saver whose reading mode costs $12.99/month and whose fact sheet records no bulk import at all is a hard sell as a read-it-later app.

## What separates a great read-it-later app in 2027

1. **Capture friction.** One click on desktop, share-sheet on mobile, an email address for newsletters, and a browser you actually use.
2. **Reader quality.** Clean parse, reliable typography, graceful failure on script-heavy or paywalled pages. Parsing decides daily satisfaction.
3. **Retrieval.** Keyword search requires you to remember the words; semantic search only requires you to remember the idea. Of the nine tools below, semantic retrieval exists in Marqly (Pro) and Karakeep (with your own model or key), and nowhere else at any price.
4. **Offline and audio.** Per-device caches and real sync are different things, and video rarely comes along. Audio splits into genuine text-to-speech (Matter, Instapaper Premium) and transcribed video (Matter, Marqly, Readwise Reader).
5. **Durability.** Can you get everything out in a documented format without asking anyone, and does the revenue model depend on anything other than a parent company's mood?

Every tool below wins something and is limited on exactly one of these axes. We name the limiting axis for each, because that is the axis to shop on.

## 3. Raindrop.io — best free all-rounder

Raindrop is the most generous free tier in the category and the safest default for anyone who does not want to pay: **unlimited bookmarks, collections and devices, up to three highlights per bookmark, plus import and export**, with no expiry and no card. Apps cover web, Mac, Windows, Linux, iOS and Android with extensions for Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari — the broadest platform reach here.

Where it stops short is retrieval and reading. Search on free is titles and tags; **full-text search requires Pro** and is keyword-shaped even then. There is **no offline mode** — pages are cached and Pro keeps permanent copies, but the fact sheet records no offline reading, a real gap for commuters and a correction to our 2026 table.

Pro is cheap and worth it for archiving alone: **$28/year or $3.25/month** adds full-text search across saved pages and PDFs, Stella AI suggestions for tags and collections at save time, permanent page copies, annotations with unlimited highlights in four colours, reminders, a duplicate finder and daily backups. Stella is a save-time assistant, not a retrieval engine — it suggests a tag, it does not find an article you have forgotten.

**Who it's best for:** collectors who want a free, dependable, cross-platform filing cabinet and will organise by hand. **Skip it if** retrieval is your pain, since no tier changes the keyword-only model, or you need offline reading on a plane.

- **Pros:** the most generous free tier here; apps on every platform including Linux; polished visual organisation with nested collections; public and collaborative collections; cheap Pro with permanent page copies and daily backups; open API.
- **Cons:** no semantic search at any price; full-text search and annotations beyond three highlights are paywalled; no offline reading; basic reader; organisation entirely manual.
- **Price:** free (unlimited) · Pro $28/year or $3.25/month · App Store pricing $27.99/year or $2.99/month.

It reaches web, iOS, Android, Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Kindle, and after Pocket's shutdown it added a direct Pocket import.

Two corrections matter, because much advice about Instapaper is stale. **Premium is $5.99/month or $59.99/year**; it roughly doubled in late 2023 from $3/month and $30/year, so any article still quoting three dollars describes a price that no longer exists. **Who it's best for:** minimalists who read within a week of saving, Kindle owners, and anyone migrating a Pocket export who wants the least app possible. **Skip it if** you are trying to make a big backlog findable, or want audio without paying. ## 5. Wallabag — best if durability is the whole point

Wallabag is open source under **MIT**, free to self-host, and maintained continuously since it started in France in 2013. That combination is the direct answer to the Pocket and Omnivore lesson: no company can decide to shut it down, and if the project stalls, a working copy is already on your server. It is a read-it-later tool first, so it reproduces the behaviour people miss — save a page, read a clean copy later, on web, iOS, Android, Chrome or Firefox.

It is also the cheapest paid option here. Official **wallabag.it** hosting is **€11/year** with every feature on every plan, plus a €4 three-month option and a €30/year supporter plan; hosted accounts get a 14-day trial with no card. Export is comprehensive both ways — JSON, CSV, ePub, PDF and plain text out, with imports from Pocket, Instapaper, Readability and browser bookmark HTML, and a full REST API. ePub export is the sleeper feature and the reason many e-reader owners never leave.

The trade is polish and intelligence. The interface is functional, parsing can fail on script-heavy sites, self-hosting means running PHP, a database and updates, and there is **no AI of any kind**. Tagging can be rule-based and automatic, which is genuinely useful, but the rules are yours to write.

**Who it's best for:** anyone whose primary 2027 criterion is "this will still exist in 2030," plus e-reader owners and technical users. **Skip it if** you want AI retrieval or a polished native app; our [self-hosted Pocket alternative](/blog/best-self-hosted-pocket-alternative) guide compares the open-source field.

- **Pros:** MIT-licensed and free to self-host; 13 years of continuous maintenance; €11/year hosted with no feature gating; ePub and PDF export; rule-based automatic tagging; full REST API; imports from Pocket and Instapaper.
- **Cons:** no AI features at all; utilitarian design; parsing can fail on complex pages; self-hosting is real setup and ongoing maintenance.
- **Price:** free self-hosted · €11/year on wallabag.it · €4 for three months · €30/year supporter plan.

The limits are platform and retrieval — **no Android, no Windows, no web version, no browser extension**, keyword search only, manual tags, no semantic retrieval.

**Who it's best for:** Apple-only readers who want a fast, private, subscription-free queue with strong automation and permanent article copies. **Skip it if** you use a non-Apple device, need a browser extension, or want your library findable by meaning.

## 8. Karakeep — best self-hosted option with real AI

Formerly Hoarder, renamed in early 2025, it is free and open source to self-host with every feature included, and an LLM you configure — any OpenAI-compatible key, or a local Ollama model at no cost — **auto-tags everything and writes summaries**, with Meilisearch full-text search plus **semantic and hybrid modes added in 2026**. The model can run locally, so the AI can be private as well as free.

It is also a serious archive: full-page preservation stores a page copy, a screenshot and a PDF, and can pull video with yt-dlp, so link rot cannot take your library. Extensions ship for Chrome, Firefox and Safari, iOS and Android apps have offline reading, public lists have RSS feeds, and a REST API, webhooks, CLI and MCP server let you wire it in. Imports cover Pocket, browser HTML, Omnivore, Instapaper, Linkwarden and mymind.

Count the costs before committing: Docker, updates and backups, plus token costs or a local GPU. There is **no built-in AI Q&A over your saves and no YouTube AI**, and no one-click save-all-tabs. The hosted Karakeep Cloud is in public beta with a **$0 tier capped at 10 bookmarks** and a **$4/month** Pro tier — ten bookmarks is a preview, so "free Karakeep" means your own server.

**Who it's best for:** technical readers who want AI organisation without handing a vendor their library. **Skip it if** you do not want to administer software, or you want audio and YouTube handling.

## 9. mymind — best for visual saving, if you never need to import

mymind is the most beautiful saving tool here and the one whose fact sheet argues hardest against ranking it as a read-it-later app. Everything you save — bookmarks, images, quotes, notes, PDFs — lands as a card on one searchable visual canvas, with no folders and no manual filing. AI tags each card automatically and recognises objects, colours, brands and text inside images, so visual saves become searchable by description. It is deliberately single-player: no ads, no tracking, no social features.

The disqualifiers for this query are structural. **There is no bulk import by design** — no Pocket archive, no Raindrop export, no browser bookmarks — and the read/write API that entered beta in mid-2026 means writing code. **Nothing can be shared.** There are no highlights and no offline reading. And the tier a reader needs is the expensive one: the free guest plan caps at **100 cards**, The Bookmarker at **$4.99/month has no AI at all**, AI tagging starts at Student of Life (**$7.99/month or $79/year**), and **AI summaries, reading mode, article backup and the PDF analyzer require Mastermind at $12.99/month or $129/year**.

Export is a one-way CSV of cards plus your files, and it works only from desktop Chrome or Edge, with no re-import format.

**Who it's best for:** designers and visual thinkers starting fresh, who save more images than articles and value privacy and aesthetics over organisation. **Skip it if** you have a library to move, need to share a reading list, want offline reading, or want reading mode without the top tier. See [Marqly vs mymind](/compare/marqly-vs-mymind).

- **Pros:** the best-looking canvas in the category; AI recognises content inside images; zero-filing philosophy; hard privacy stance; apps on web, Mac, iOS and Android with Chrome, Edge and Safari extensions; read/write API in beta since mid-2026.
- **Cons:** no bulk import by design; nothing shareable; reading mode and AI summaries sit in the $12.99/month tier; no highlights and no offline reading; free guest plan caps at 100 cards; export is one-way CSV from desktop Chrome or Edge only.
- **Price:** free guest plan capped at 100 cards · The Bookmarker $4.99/month · Student of Life $7.99/month or $79/year · Mastermind $12.99/month or $129/year.

## Also tested, and why they did not make the top nine

- **Notion Web Clipper.** Free, and right if your notes already live in Notion — clips land in databases and inherit properties and views, and offline mode arrived in 2025 on all plans. It misses because the clipper is bare-bones at capture (no tags, notes or highlights), Notion's own docs warn clip fidelity varies, browser support has narrowed to Chrome and Safari, and the AI people assume is included is a **Business-tier capability at $20/member/month** — Free and Plus ($10/member/month annual) only get a trial of it. See [Marqly vs Notion Web Clipper](/compare/marqly-vs-notion-web-clipper).
- **Evernote Web Clipper.** Works on all five major browsers and v11 in January 2026 added an AI assistant plus semantic search. It misses because the free plan is a demo — **50 notes, one notebook, one device** — so real use means Starter at $14.99/month or $99/year, or Advanced at $24.99/month or $249.99/year. It also cannot highlight live web pages.
- **Linkwarden.** AGPL-3.0 with every feature self-hosted, and the best pure preservation tool we know: each save becomes a screenshot, a PDF and a readable copy. Not a reader — no queue, no text-to-speech, no reading progress. Cloud is $3/month per user billed yearly or $4 monthly, with a 14-day trial and no free cloud tier.
- **Anybox.** A superb native Apple organiser at $14.99/year, $1.99/month or $39.99 lifetime, with a 50-link free tier — but no AI, no web-page highlighting, no full-text search inside archives, and no web or Android version.
- **Pocket.** Mozilla shut it down on July 8, 2025; export access and the API were disabled on November 12, 2025 and remaining data was queued for permanent deletion from that date. It is `status: shutdown` in our data layer and is not a recommendation.
- **Omnivore and its forks.** Shut down in November 2024 after its team joined ElevenLabs, with an export deadline of November 30, 2024. The AGPL code survives, but the stack needs PostgreSQL, Elasticsearch and multiple microservices, and the forks are volunteer-maintained with no guarantees. ## How to move a reading library without losing it

1. **Export in the right file.** Rescuing a Pocket archive means using the **`list.csv` inside the export ZIP** — not the HTML preview file, whose `<ul><li>` layout is not the Netscape bookmark format and does not parse. We measured **0 of 261 items** from a genuine export; method in the [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity). From Raindrop use Settings → Backups → Export HTML, since its JSON export is not accepted everywhere.
2. **Clean before importing.** Importing 4,000 links of which 900 are dead just relocates the problem. The free [Duplicate Bookmark Finder](/tools/duplicate-bookmark-finder) and [Dead Link Checker](/tools/dead-link-checker) handle both; the [Bookmark File Viewer](/tools/bookmark-file-viewer) and [Pocket Export Converter](/tools/pocket-export-converter) tell you what you hold.
3. **Check file limits.** Marqly's importer takes `.html`, `.htm` and `.csv` up to **10 MB on free and 30 MB on Pro**, with **10,000 bookmarks per file**.
4. **Know what carries across.** Titles, URLs, descriptions and tags do, and folders become boards flattened to at most two levels. **Original save dates do not** — imported items take the import date — and highlights, article text and read status never survive any importer. No deduplication runs during import.
5. **Budget for tagging, or let Pro do it.** Auto-tagging an import is Pro; on free your existing tags come across and nothing new is added.
6. **Test the exit on day one.** Export something back out and confirm it is readable. Marqly's CSV button sits in the sidebar and profile menu and carries URL, title, description and tags; a complete account copy including highlights, notes and boards comes from support@marqly.com.

Tool-specific steps live in the [Pocket migration guide](/migrate/pocket), the [Instapaper guide](/migrate/instapaper) and the [Migration Center](/migrate).

## What actually matters in 2027

**Retrieval beats capture.** Pocket proved that a delightful save button plus title-and-URL search produces a library nobody uses. Every tool here saves well; only two retrieve by meaning.

**Durability is a ten-minute check.** Two category leaders vanished within eight months, one of them free with no revenue model. Ask whether you can get everything out in a documented format without asking anyone, whether the business depends on subscribers rather than a parent company's portfolio review, and whether the code is open source so a shutdown leaves a fork instead of a hole. Wallabag, Karakeep, Linkwarden and GoodLinks score best by construction. Every hosted tool is weaker, including ours.

**Offline and audio are where the fine print lives.** "Supports offline" hides per-device caches with no server copy and tiers that gate it. "Has audio" hides the gap between real narration and a system voice. And if you buy through an app store, check the web price first — Matter's annual plan is $20 cheaper there.
