2. **Readwise Reader was marked as having semantic search.** It does not. Our fact sheet is explicit: *"search is keyword-based rather than semantic."* Reader has Ghostreader AI for summarising documents, defining terms and answering questions about a text, plus full-text search and synced YouTube transcripts — which is a lot, and none of it is meaning-based retrieval. 8. **Instapaper's own feature set was under-described.** It has a documented developer API, highlight sync out to Readwise, and export on the free plan. It does not have browser-bookmark HTML import, public sharing, or an Edge extension.

## When to leave Instapaper — and when to stay

**Stay** if all you ever want is to save an article and read it cleanly. Instapaper still does that better than anything on this list: eighteen years of typography and parser tuning, offline reading on iOS and Android, scheduled send-to-Kindle delivery, and a permanent archive on Premium. It is also cheaper than four of the seven alternatives below over five years. There is nothing wrong with simple, and there is nothing wrong with cheap.

**Leave** if you have noticed any of these:

- **You save things and never find them again.** This is the big one, and it compounds: the longer you use a retrieval-less tool, the larger the unsearchable pile gets.
- **You want to search by what you *remember*, not by exact words.** Instapaper's search is keyword-literal, and it is Premium-only. Free accounts cannot full-text search their own saves at all.
- **You want summaries to triage a backlog.** Instapaper has no AI summarisation at any price.
- **You want the filing done for you.** Organisation is manual folders. No tags, no automatic collections, nothing resurfaced.
- **You want your reading to become a searchable knowledge base.** That requires a retrieval layer, and Instapaper does not have one.
- **You need to publish a list to someone else** — a class, a book club, a colleague, a newsletter audience. Instapaper has no public sharing of any kind.
- **You listen more than you read and want good voices.** Instapaper's Premium text-to-speech is decent. Matter's HD voices are the reason Matter exists.
- **You want to own the software.** After two shutdowns, this is a reasonable position, and Instapaper is closed-source and hosted.
- **You live in Microsoft Edge.** No Edge extension.
- **You save things that are not articles.** Videos, images, reference links and products all sit awkwardly in a queue built for prose.

If none of those landed, close this tab and keep Instapaper. It is a good tool and this page is not an argument that it is bad — it is an argument that "calm" and "findable" are different goals, and Instapaper has only ever pursued the first.

It is also the only tool here where the organisational work is done for you and the retrieval is meaning-based at the same time. Karakeep can do both, but only on your own server with your own model. Everything else on this list does one or neither.

## 2. Readwise Reader — best for heavy readers who annotate

If Instapaper's minimalism feels like a straightjacket rather than a relief, Readwise Reader is the other end of the spectrum. It folds **articles, RSS feeds, email newsletters, PDFs, ePubs, Twitter threads and YouTube videos** into one triage-oriented inbox, and its Ghostreader assistant uses AI to **summarise documents, define terms and answer questions against the text**. YouTube videos arrive with synced transcripts you can highlight like an article. It is keyboard-first, dense, and the fastest tool in the category once you have paid the learning cost.

The reason it is ranked second rather than first is the part that actually decides whether you should buy it: Reader is the **annotation pipeline**, not the retrieval layer. Everything you highlight flows into the core Readwise loop — daily spaced-repetition review, plus sync out to Notion, Obsidian, Evernote and Roam. If your reading is research and your output is notes in another tool, nothing here competes. If your problem is "I cannot find the article," Reader's search is keyword-based, its tagging is manual, and it will not fix that.

It is also the most expensive option on this list by a clear margin, and the pricing structure is unusual: **Reader only ships with the Readwise Full plan**, so you pay for Readwise highlight syncing whether you use it or not.

**Who it's best for:** serious readers and note-takers consuming large volumes across articles, newsletters, PDFs and video, who want highlights flowing into Notion or Obsidian. **Skip it if** you are a casual reader (the UI is dense), you want a free tier (there is no permanent one), you want semantic retrieval, or you want to publish collections publicly.

- **Pros:** every content type in one inbox; Ghostreader AI summaries and Q&A over the open text; synced YouTube transcripts; best-in-class highlight workflow with daily review; exports to Notion, Obsidian and Roam; documented API; active, well-funded development.
- **Cons:** no permanent free tier; Reader requires the Full plan, so you buy highlight syncing whether or not you want it; dense UI with a real learning curve; no public sharing; tagging is manual; **search is keyword-based rather than semantic**; no Safari or Edge extension.
- **Price:** **$9.99/month billed annually — $119.88/year — or $12.99/month billed monthly**, after 30 days free. The cheaper **Lite** plan at $5.59/month billed annually covers classic Readwise highlight syncing *without* the Reader app. [Readwise Reader vs Marqly](/compare/marqly-vs-readwise-reader) has the row-by-row.

## 3. Matter — best for listening, if you are on Apple hardware

Matter is the design-forward read-later app for iPhone, iPad, Mac and the web, and it has the **strongest text-to-speech in the category** — human-like voices with word-by-word tracking, so you can switch between reading and listening mid-article without losing your place. That single capability is the reason to choose it, and it is genuinely better than Instapaper's Premium narration.

It is more than an audio app. Parsing is excellent, newsletters arrive via Gmail integration or a dedicated Matter email address, and **YouTube videos and podcasts are transcribed with time-synced text you can highlight**. The free tier is unusually good: an unlimited library, share-sheet saving, a Chrome extension and unlimited tags. Premium adds HD voices, unlimited highlighting and notes, offline full-text search, and integrations with Notion, Obsidian and Readwise.

The disqualifiers are blunt and worth reading before you fall for the demo. **There is no Android app.** There is **no public API and no public sharing** of collections. And the App Store annual price is **$79.99 against $60 on the web** — a 33% markup for the convenience of Apple's billing that a lot of subscribers never notice. Organisation is manual: no AI summaries, no chat, no automatic tagging.

**Who it's best for:** Apple-ecosystem readers who listen as much as they read — commuters and audio-first users who want articles, newsletters and podcasts in one queue. **Skip it if** you use Android, you want an API, you want AI organisation, or you want to pay the lower annual price without noticing which store you subscribed through.

- **Pros:** the best listening experience in the category; beautiful, focused reading design on iPhone and iPad with Apple Pencil highlighting; newsletter ingestion via Gmail or a dedicated address; time-synced YouTube and podcast transcripts you can highlight; solid free tier with an unlimited library and unlimited tags; highlights sync to Notion, Obsidian and Readwise.
- **Cons:** no Android app; no public API; no public sharing; HD voices, unlimited highlighting and full-text search all sit behind Premium; no AI summaries, chat or automatic tagging; App Store annual billing costs $19.99 more than the web.
- **Price:** free tier with an unlimited library. Premium **$8/month or $60/year on the web**; **$7.99/month or $79.99/year through the App Store**. A higher Matter Pro tier at $12.99/month also appears in the App Store. [Instapaper vs Matter](/compare/instapaper-vs-matter).

## 4. Raindrop.io — best free library for visual collectors

Raindrop is the answer if the thing you resent about Instapaper is not the reading experience but the filing, and if you would rather not pay at all. Its free plan is the most generous hosted tier in the category: **unlimited bookmarks, collections and devices**, with up to three highlights per bookmark and 100 MB/month of uploads. No expiry, no card, no volume cliff.

It is a bookmark manager rather than a reader, which cuts both ways. It saves every media type — articles, images, videos, products — into structured collections with a visual polish Instapaper does not attempt, and it runs everywhere: **web, Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari, iOS, Android, macOS, Windows and Linux**. Ten platforms, the widest coverage on this list. Pro adds full-text search, permanent page copies, Stella AI save-time tag and collection suggestions, reminders, unlimited highlights in four colours with notes, annotations and daily backups.

What it does not have is an intelligence layer. Stella makes *suggestions at save time*; there is no automatic tagging applied to your library, no AI summaries, no semantic search and no chat. Retrieval still depends on your own tags and keywords — which is a better position than Instapaper's, because at least the tags are yours to build, but not a fundamentally different one. And there is **no offline reading mode**.

**Who it's best for:** collectors and visual organisers filing thousands of bookmarks into structured collections who want polished apps everywhere without paying. **Skip it if** you want a reading queue with typography (Instapaper or Matter), semantic retrieval (Marqly or Karakeep), offline reading, or automatic filing.

- **Pros:** the best free tier here — unlimited bookmarks, collections and devices with no expiry; ten platforms including every major browser and desktop OS; saves all media types; public and shared collections; permanent page copies on Pro; full-text search on Pro; documented API; imports from Pocket and browser bookmark HTML.
- **Cons:** no semantic search and no AI summaries or chat; Stella is save-time suggestion only, not automatic tagging; **no offline reading mode**; free plan limits highlights to three per bookmark and uploads to 100 MB/month; retrieval depends on your own tagging discipline.

## 5. Wallabag — best for ownership at the lowest possible price

Wallabag is the open-source read-it-later app that has been maintained continuously since **2013**, started in France, and quietly outlived better-funded competitors. You can run it on your own server for free, or use the official hosted service at **wallabag.it for €11 a year** — among the cheapest paid plans anywhere in software, not just in this category. A €4 three-month option and a €30/year supporter plan round it out, and hosted accounts get 14 days free with no card.

The feature that wins it a permanent place on this list is **ePub export**, alongside PDF and other formats. If you read on a Kobo or another e-reader, Wallabag is the only tool here that sends your saves to the device natively, and it is why a lot of e-reader owners never leave. It also has **rule-based automatic tagging** — deterministic rather than AI, but genuinely hands-off — annotations on selected text, RSS feeds of your saves, public share links for individual articles, a full REST API, and importers covering **Pocket, Instapaper, Readability and browser bookmark files**. It is one of only two tools on this list that will import an Instapaper library directly.

The trade-off is stated plainly by its own fact sheet: **the interface is functional rather than beautiful**, parsing can fail or render poorly on complex, script-heavy sites, there are **no AI features of any kind**, and self-hosting requires real setup and ongoing maintenance across PHP, a database and updates.

**Who it's best for:** privacy-focused readers and self-hosters who want a Pocket-style queue under their own control, plus e-reader owners who export to ePub. **Skip it if** polish affects whether you open the app, you want AI, or you have no appetite for running a PHP stack.

- **Pros:** open source and free to self-host; **€11/year** official hosting that funds development, with every feature on every plan; exports to PDF, ePub and other formats; rule-based automatic tagging; imports from Pocket, Instapaper and browser bookmark files; native iOS and Android apps with offline reading; public share links; full REST API; thirteen years of continuous maintenance.
- **Cons:** utilitarian design that feels dated next to commercial rivals; parsing can fail on complex, script-heavy sites; no AI of any kind — no summaries, no semantic search, no smart tagging; self-hosting means PHP, a database and ongoing updates.
- **Price:** free self-hosted. **€11/year** on wallabag.it, or €4 for three months, or €30/year for the supporter plan, with 14 days free and no card. [Wallabag alternatives](/alternatives/wallabag) · [Instapaper vs Wallabag](/compare/instapaper-vs-wallabag).

## 6. Karakeep — best for self-hosters who want real AI

Karakeep — formerly Hoarder, renamed in early 2025 — is what happens when the self-hosted crowd builds an AI bookmark manager. Self-hosting is **fully free and open source with no volume cap**, and the AI is bring-your-own: point it at any **OpenAI-compatible API key** or at a **local Ollama model**, and it auto-tags and summarises everything you save. It added **semantic and hybrid search modes during 2026** on top of a Meilisearch-backed full-text index, which makes it the only genuinely free path to meaning-based retrieval on this list.

The rest of the product is unusually complete for open source. Full-page archiving writes a page copy, a screenshot and a PDF for every link, with yt-dlp for video. Extensions ship for **Chrome, Firefox and Safari** — no Edge — and iOS and Android apps gained offline reading in 2026. Public lists have RSS feeds. And the automation surface is a **REST API, webhooks, a CLI and an MCP server**, so an AI agent can read and act on your library directly. Imports cover Pocket, browser bookmark HTML, Omnivore, Instapaper, Linkwarden and mymind — two of those six services no longer exist, which tells you who Karakeep is built for.

The costs are the self-hosted costs. You are the sysadmin, permanently: Docker, updates, backups you actually test, reverse-proxy security, and disk space that fills faster than you expect once every save writes three copies. The AI is free only on the Ollama path; a frontier API meters per thousand tokens. Karakeep is still pre-1.0. There is **no AI Q&A over your saves** and **no YouTube-specific AI**. And Karakeep Cloud, the hosted escape hatch, is in public beta with a **$0 tier capped at 10 bookmarks** and Pro at **$4/month** — no published annual price, and ten bookmarks cannot tell you what living with it is like.

**Who it's best for:** self-hosters and privacy-focused tinkerers who want AI tagging, summaries and semantic search on their own hardware without a subscription. **Skip it if** you have no always-on machine, you live in Edge, you want to ask your library questions, or you want someone else to hold the pager.

- **Pros:** free, open source and self-hosted with all features and no caps; AI auto-tagging and summaries via any OpenAI-compatible API or fully local Ollama; semantic and hybrid search added in 2026; full-page archiving plus yt-dlp video; iOS and Android apps with offline reading plus Chrome, Firefox and Safari extensions; REST API, webhooks, CLI and an MCP server; imports from six sources including dead ones.
- **Cons:** self-hosting requires Docker and bring-your-own AI keys; Cloud is public beta with a 10-bookmark free tier and no published annual price; no AI Q&A over your saves and no YouTube AI; no Edge extension; no one-click save-all-tabs; pre-1.0 turbulence; archives fill disks.
- **Price:** free self-hosted. Karakeep Cloud: **$0 tier capped at 10 bookmarks**, **Pro $4/month**, public beta. See our full [Karakeep review for 2027](/blog/karakeep-review-2027) and [Marqly vs Karakeep](/compare/marqly-vs-karakeep).

## 7. Linkwarden — best for making sure the links still work

Linkwarden is the archivist's answer, and it solves a problem none of the other six treat as central: **the page behind your bookmark will change or disappear.** Every saved link is automatically preserved as a **screenshot, a PDF and a readable text copy**, and Linkwarden can also submit it to the **Wayback Machine**. Instapaper's permanent archive does something similar behind Premium; Linkwarden does it in more formats, self-hosted, for free.

It is also the most collaborative tool on this list by a distance. Collections nest, can be **shared with teammates with per-member permissions**, and can be **published as public pages**. For a research group, a content team, a classroom or a shared reading list, that is a capability Instapaper simply does not have. Self-hosting is free under **AGPL-3.0 with all features included**; Cloud is **$3/month per user billed yearly — 25% off the $4/month monthly price — with up to 30,000 links per user**, expandable via support, and no permanent free tier beyond 14 days.

The limits are structural rather than incidental. **Search is keyword and full-text only — there is no semantic search.** The AI **tags and does nothing else**: no summaries, no Q&A, nothing resurfaced. It is **built for collecting and preserving rather than reading**, with no reader queue, no text-to-speech and no reading progress. Extensions cover **Chrome, Firefox and Edge** — no Safari. Multi-format archiving means a headless browser doing captures and storage that grows fast, so a self-hosted instance wants a real server.

**Who it's best for:** researchers, journalists, lawyers and citation-heavy writers who need pages as they were, plus teams sharing curated collections with permissions. **Skip it if** your problem is retrieval or triage, you read in your bookmark app, you live in Safari, or you want a free hosted account.

- **Pros:** automatic screenshot, PDF and readable copy for every link, plus Wayback Machine submission; AGPL-3.0 with genuine self-host parity and no held-back features; shared collections with per-member permissions and public pages; optional AI tagging including local models; full-text search across archived content; documented REST API; official iOS and Android apps with offline caching; $36/year per user on Cloud.
- **Cons:** no semantic search, no summaries, no Q&A; no reader queue, no text-to-speech, no reading progress; no free Cloud tier; no Safari extension; storage-hungry by design; mobile apps still catching up to the web.
- **Price:** free self-hosted. Cloud **$3/month per user billed yearly (25% off)** or **$4/month billed monthly**, 14 days free, up to 30,000 links per user. See our full [Linkwarden review for 2027](/blog/linkwarden-review-2027), [Linkwarden vs Karakeep](/compare/linkwarden-vs-karakeep) and [Marqly vs Linkwarden](/compare/marqly-vs-linkwarden).

## Durability: two shutdowns reshaped this list

This is the section the 2026 edition could not have written, and it is the reason the ranking below differs from a pure feature comparison.

**Mozilla shut Pocket down on July 8, 2025.** Export access and the API were disabled on **November 12, 2025**, data deletion began then, and Premium subscribers received prorated refunds. **Omnivore shut down in November 2024** after its team was acqui-hired by ElevenLabs, with an export deadline of **November 30, 2024**. Both are `status: "shutdown"` in our data layer. Pocket had been the default recommendation for a decade and was owned by a foundation. Omnivore was free, open source and widely beloved. Neither survived, and neither death was predictable from the product.

So "will this still exist" is now a legitimate column, and here is how the field scores on the four things we can actually verify — licence, revenue model, export path and import path:

| Tool | Licence / code | Revenue model | Export you can rely on | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Instapaper** | Closed | Premium since launch; independent since 2018 | **HTML or CSV on the free plan**, developer API, Readwise highlight sync | **Strongest survival record** — 18 years, three owners, no data disaster |
| **Wallabag** | Open source | €11/yr hosted funds development | PDF, ePub and other formats; full REST API | **Strongest ownership record** — running continuously since 2013 |
| **Karakeep** | Open source | Cloud, public beta | Karakeep JSON or standard Netscape HTML, lists included | Strong — a stalled project still leaves a working install |
| **Linkwarden** | **AGPL-3.0, all features self-hosted** | Cloud subscription | Standard bookmark HTML or JSON, downloadable archives, REST API | Strong — no open-core cripple, best portability here |
| **Raindrop.io** | Closed | Free tier + Pro | Bookmark HTML export; permanent copies on Pro | Good — mature, funded, but closed |
| **Marqly** | Closed | Free tier + Pro + Teams | **CSV export button** — URL, title, description, tags; complete account copy via support | Good — real self-serve export, but the CSV does not carry highlights, notes or boards |
| **Readwise Reader** | Closed | Readwise Full subscription | Highlights and notes sync out to Notion, Obsidian, Evernote, Roam; documented API | Good — the annotation export path is the best anywhere |
| **Matter** | Closed | Free tier + Premium | Highlights sync to Notion, Obsidian, Readwise; **no public API** | Weakest — no API and no public sharing means fewer ways out |

Two honest notes on our own row. Marqly's CSV export carries **URL, title, description and tags — nothing else**, so it does not carry highlights, notes or boards; for a complete account copy you email support@marqly.com. On the free plan that CSV covers your newest 100 items, and Pro exports the whole library with an option to export only what is new since the last export. And Marqly is closed-source with no self-hosted option, which means its durability rests on the company rather than on a licence. We are not going to claim the open-source tools' advantage here.

The practical conclusion is not "pick open source." It is **test your exit before you need it.** Export a small library on the day you adopt a tool, open the file, and check whether the thing you actually care about — links, highlights, notes, archives — is in it. Our [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity) exists because we ran that test on ourselves: measured on September 26, 2026, **0 of 261 items parsed** from a Pocket export's HTML preview file and **43 of 43** parsed from a Chrome bookmark export. The file you feed an importer matters more than the importer's reputation, and the same is true in reverse when you leave.

## How to move your Instapaper library

Instapaper's export is available on the **free** plan, which is better than most tools in this category manage. The steps:

1. **Export on the web, not the app.** In Instapaper's account settings, export your saved articles. You get a choice of **HTML or CSV**. The HTML is a standard bookmarks file that most read-it-later and bookmarking apps import directly; the CSV is easier to inspect or clean up first.
2. **Get your highlights out separately, before anything else.** The export carries your links — not your annotations. If a decade of highlighting is the real library, connect **Readwise** and let it sync your Instapaper highlights and notes out first. This is the step people skip and then regret.
3. **Check what you are actually holding.** Open the file. If you want to inspect or de-duplicate it before committing it anywhere, our [Bookmark File Viewer](/tools/bookmark-file-viewer) and [Duplicate Bookmark Finder](/tools/duplicate-bookmark-finder) both run in the browser from the [free tools directory](/tools).
4. **Import into the destination.** Marqly's [Migration Center](/migrate) takes browser bookmark HTML, Raindrop HTML collections, generic CSV, and the `list.csv` from inside a Pocket export archive. Wallabag imports Instapaper directly, which is the shortest path on this list. Karakeep's importer list covers Instapaper too.
5. **Expect to lose some things, and know which.** Across every importer we have tested, the fields that survive are title, URL, description or note, tags, and folder structure. The fields that routinely do not are original save dates, highlights, article text, and read status. Folder trees get flattened — in Marqly's case to at most two board levels. Our [import-fidelity study](/research/bookmark-import-fidelity) documents the measured version of this rather than the promised version.
6. **Keep the export file.** Not for a week — indefinitely. It is the only copy of your Instapaper library that does not depend on Instapaper, and if the permanent archive matters to you, that archive is the one thing Premium cannot hand back when you leave.
7. **Do not delete your Instapaper account on day one.** Run the new tool alongside it for a fortnight. Most migration regret is about workflow rather than data, and workflow is only visible after two weeks of real use.

## Why retrieval beats minimalism in 2027

Instapaper's minimalism is not a mistake, and it is not going away. It is a coherent bet that the valuable part of a saved article is the reading of it, and that everything else — tags, recommendations, intelligence layers, social surfaces — is friction between you and the text. For a person who saves an article and reads it that week, that bet pays. It has paid for eighteen years.

The bet stops paying when the pile outgrows the reading. At some point the library stops being a queue and becomes an archive, and an archive you cannot search is not an archive — it is storage. This is the same failure mode that turned Pocket into a graveyard for millions of people who only discovered the problem when Mozilla gave them a four-month window to export. Instapaper's free tier accelerates it, because a free account cannot full-text search its own saves; the pile grows and the retrieval stays at zero until you pay.

That is why this list is ranked the way it is, and why the top row is the one with the retrieval layer.
