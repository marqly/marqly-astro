## The four things that actually decide a web highlighter

1. **Persistence.** Does the highlight reappear on the live page when you return, or does it only exist inside the tool? Persistence is what makes highlighting a reading habit rather than an archival one.
2. **Colours, notes and privacy.** How many colours, whether notes attach to highlights, whether notes are capped, and — the one people discover late — **whether your highlights are public by default**.
3. **Export.** Can you leave with your work in a format another tool reads, without asking anyone? This is the axis with the widest spread in the category, from "already plain files on your disk" to "no export advertised."
4. **Price and platform reach.** What the free tier actually includes, what the paid tier costs, and whether the extension exists for the browser and phone you use.

Every tool below wins at least one of these and is limited on exactly one. We name the limiting axis in each section, because that is the axis to shop on.

## 2. Glasp — best for sharing what you highlight, and best export pipeline

Glasp is a social highlighter: your highlights build a public profile others can follow, closer to Goodreads for web reading than to a private notebook. Core highlighting is **free and unlimited**, with multiple colours and note support, and AI-suggested tags help organise saves. It also makes the widely used YouTube Summary extension, and paid plans add advanced YouTube summaries, channel tracking, PDF chat, audio transcription and an AI clone that answers questions from your highlights.

Its two strongest verified credentials are reach and exit. **Platform coverage is the broadest in this niche** — Chrome, Safari, Edge and Firefox extensions plus iOS and Android apps rebuilt in 2026, including a mobile Safari extension almost no competitor offers. And export is the best here: **Markdown, CSV, HTML and JSON**, with Notion auto-sync, an official Obsidian plugin, Readwise, Roam and Logseq integrations, dedicated Kindle highlight import and export, and a developer API with an MCP connector.

The caveats are real. **Free highlights are public by default** — keeping them private requires Pro. Free AI caps are tight: three YouTube summaries a day, 30 PDF uploads and 20 PDF chat messages a month. **Pro is $15/month or $150/year** and the Unlimited tier is **$36/month or $360/year**, after prices rose about 20–25% in May 2026, and subscriptions are explicitly non-refundable. Students get a discount on Pro yearly with an academic email. There is no full-text search, no full-page archiving and no offline mode, and Glasp is run by its two co-founders, which is a genuine support and longevity risk.

**Who it's best for:** learners who want to highlight in public and discover what others underline, and anyone for whom export format is a hard requirement. **Skip it if** your highlights must stay private on a budget, or you want full-text search and archiving. See [Marqly vs Glasp](/compare/marqly-vs-glasp).

## 3. Readwise Reader — best for export and spaced repetition

Reader is the power reader's stack: a read-it-later app, RSS reader and highlighter in one, with the Readwise review system underneath. Highlight on the web via the extension, in PDFs, in ePubs, in newsletters and in YouTube transcripts — everything lands in one place, then flows out to Notion, Obsidian, Evernote and Roam. The **daily spaced-repetition review** resurfaces old highlights so you actually retain them, which is a genuinely different idea from anything else in this category. Ghostreader summarises documents and answers questions against the text.

It is also the most portable of the commercial options: highlights and notes export continuously in Markdown, your document list exports as CSV, and a documented public API gives programmatic access to everything. If you cancel, you can take the library and the annotations with you in open formats.

The costs are price, complexity and platform. There is **no permanent free tier** — Readwise offers a 30-day trial, after which a subscription is required — and Reader ships **only with the Full plan** at $9.99/month billed annually ($119.88/year) or $12.99/month monthly; the cheaper Lite plan at $5.59/month billed annually covers classic highlight syncing without Reader. Verified students and educators get 50% off. It is a whole reading workflow with a real learning curve, and the Reader app's own browser support is Chrome and Firefox — no Safari or Edge extension, though the separate Readwise aggregator does reach those.

**Who it's best for:** heavy readers whose highlights need to end up in a note system and be resurfaced. **Skip it if** you just want to mark up pages as you browse, you want free, or you need semantic search — Reader's search is keyword-based and its tagging manual. See [Marqly vs Readwise Reader](/compare/marqly-vs-readwise-reader).

- **Pros:** the best export pipeline among the paid tools; highlights across web, PDF, ePub, newsletters and YouTube; spaced-repetition review that turns highlights into retained knowledge; documented public API; 50% student and educator discount.

## 6. Obsidian Web Clipper — best if you want your highlights as files you own

Obsidian's clipper is the strongest portability answer in this category, because there is no export step: **its highlighter mode captures the passages you mark as plain Markdown files in your vault**, on your own disk, readable by any text editor and importable into virtually any notes tool. No proprietary format, no lock-in, nothing to leave with. The clipper and the app are **entirely free, including commercial use**, a policy in place since February 2025.

It is also more capable than its reputation suggests. Per-site templates decide how each clip is titled, tagged and filed, pulling metadata through selector and Schema.org variables. The **Interpreter** runs natural-language prompts at clip time — summarise, extract, translate — using your own API key from Anthropic, OpenAI or Gemini, or a local Ollama model at no cost. It runs on Chrome, Firefox, Edge and Safari, **including Safari on iPhone and iPad**, which almost no competitor offers, and it shipped versions in April, May, June and July 2026.

The costs are assembly and retrieval. **There is no sync out of the box** — cross-device means Obsidian Sync from $4/month billed annually ($5 monthly) or a DIY route like iCloud — and public sharing means Publish at $8/site/month. There is **no automatic tagging and no semantic search**; retrieval is text matching unless you wire up your own model and pay its usage. And highlights become files rather than paint: the clipper does not repaint your marks on the live page when you return to it, so if persistence-on-the-page is your primary criterion, this is the wrong tool.

**Who it's best for:** local-first purists and Obsidian users who want web passages as portable Markdown they own forever. **Skip if** you want highlights to reappear on the live page, you do not want to configure your own AI, or you need cross-device sync without paying for it.

- **Pros:** entirely free and open source including commercial use; highlights become plain Markdown with zero lock-in; highlighter mode captures only marked passages; per-site templates; Interpreter AI with your own key or free local Ollama; works on Chrome, Firefox, Edge and Safari including iOS Safari.
- **Cons:** no sync out of the box; no automatic tagging or semantic search; AI needs your own provider and usage costs; Publish costs extra; does not repaint highlights on the live page; only useful if you also run Obsidian.
- **Price:** app and clipper free · Sync from $4/month billed annually ($5 monthly) · Publish $8/site/month · your own LLM usage if you enable Interpreter.

What it also includes is **ads inside AI answers, zero agent credits and one small file upload a day**. Second, there is a **card-required trial whose length is not published**, alongside a verified pattern of Trustpilot complaints about trials auto-converting to roughly $400 annual charges and refused refunds. That is a reason to check your subscription settings the day you sign up, not a reason to avoid the product — but it is a reason to be careful. The Safari and Firefox extensions of the highlighter era are no longer offered; reach is now web, Chrome, Edge, iOS, Android, Mac and Windows.

**Who it's best for:** researchers and students who want AI search answers they can verify line by line, with Liner Scholar's literature-review and citation agents, and who treat highlighting as a bonus. **Skip it if** highlighting is the actual job, or if being unable to export your library is disqualifying — which for a long-term annotation habit it usually should be.

- **Cons:** the highlighter is no longer the product; no export advertised for saved highlights; free tier shows ads inside answers and grants zero agent credits; credits do not roll over; Safari and Firefox extensions dropped; an unpublished-length card-required trial with documented billing complaints.

## 8. Weava — best for student citations, if you accept the maintenance risk

Weava is built for research projects: it highlights both web pages and uploaded PDFs in one tool, organises everything into folders and sub-folders, and generates citations in APA, MLA, Chicago and Harvard — which is why it became a staple across more than 200 universities. The free plan is genuinely usable: **unlimited highlights and notes, folder organisation, citations and export to Word, Excel, TXT and CSV, all on free**, with 100MB of PDF storage, no sub-folders and ads. Premium is **$3.99/month billed annually or $7.99/month monthly**, adding unlimited storage, sub-folders, custom colours and invite-based collaboration, and **students get 70% off with an education email** — the deepest student discount in this article.

We ranked Weava third in 2026 and cannot defend that now. The maintenance signals are consistent and unambiguous: the **Chrome extension has not been updated since February 2024**, the website's copyright notice still reads 2022, and the **former iOS app returns 404** on the App Store as of mid-2026. Recent reviews report **disappearing highlights and folders** and slow support. Reach is **Chrome and Edge only** — no Firefox, no Safari, no working mobile apps, no API. And highlights are **tied to exact URLs**, so they vanish if a page's address changes.

None of that means Weava is broken today. It means it is maintenance-mode software, and that you should not put anything into it that you cannot afford to lose. The mitigation is cheap: export regularly to Word or CSV, which the free plan allows, and keep a second copy somewhere else.

**Who it's best for:** students on a budget who need web and PDF highlighting side by side with automatic citations, and who will keep exports. **Skip it if** you use Firefox or Safari, you need a phone app, you want AI retrieval, or you are building an annotation archive you intend to keep for years.

- **Pros:** highlights web pages and PDFs in one tool; automatic citations in four formats; unlimited highlights and notes on free; export to Word, Excel, TXT and CSV on the free plan; 70% student discount; folder and sub-folder structure suits literature reviews.
- **Cons:** Chrome extension not updated since February 2024; site copyright still reads 2022; former iOS app delisted; recent reports of disappearing highlights and folders; Chrome and Edge only with no mobile apps or API; highlights tied to exact URLs; 100MB free storage and ads; no AI of any kind.

It is an archiver rather than a highlighter, and its cloud has no free tier beyond a trial at $3/month per user billed yearly.
- **Karakeep.** Free, self-hosted, open source, with highlights and notes plus AI auto-tagging and semantic search via your own key or a local model. Excellent if you want to own the stack; it is not built for marking up pages as you browse.
- **mymind.** Despite the visual reputation, its fact sheet records **no highlighting** — text highlights appear in its export but the feature set has no highlighter. It is a card-based saver, not a markup tool.
- **Readwise (the aggregator, not Reader).** Superb at syncing highlights from Kindle, Apple Books and Twitter and resurfacing them by spaced repetition, but it cannot save or highlight web pages itself — it is the downstream half of a pipeline, and Reader is the tool that does the highlighting.

## Can you get your highlights out? Export and portability compared

This is the axis with the widest spread in the category, and the one most people discover too late.

| Tool | Self-serve export | Formats | Notes and highlights included |
|---|---|---|---|
| Obsidian Web Clipper | None needed | Plain Markdown on your disk | ✅ Everything, by construction |
| Glasp | ✅ | Markdown, CSV, HTML, JSON | ✅ Plus Notion, Obsidian, Readwise, Roam, Logseq |
| Readwise Reader | ✅ Continuous | Markdown, CSV of documents, public API | ✅ Highlights, notes and tags |
| Weava | ✅ On the free plan | Word, Excel, TXT, CSV | ✅ Plus formatted citations |
| Hypothesis | ⚠️ | API, JSON, community scripts | ✅ But not one-click |
| Raindrop.io | ✅ On the free plan | HTML, CSV | ⚠️ Links and metadata; annotations are Pro |
| Marqly | ✅ CSV button | CSV | ❌ URL, title, description, tags only |
| LINER | ❌ | None advertised | ❌ No export or import tools |

Marqly's row deserves the full detail rather than the shorthand. There is a real **CSV export button** in the sidebar and profile menu; the free plan exports your newest 100 bookmarks and Pro exports the whole library, including an option to export only what is new since your last export. That CSV carries **URL, title, description and tags — nothing else**. Highlights, notes, boards and dates are not in it. For a **complete copy of your account** including highlights, notes and boards, you email support@marqly.com and get one. On this axis Marqly loses to Obsidian, Glasp and Readwise Reader, and the honest summary is: your links are one click from portable, your highlights are one email from portable, and neither is as automatic as a Markdown file already sitting on your disk.

The lesson is the same one the last two years of shutdowns taught everyone about their reading libraries: **test the exit in the first week.** Export something, open the file, confirm your highlights are actually in it. LINER is the only tool here where that test has no passing answer.

## How to choose

1. **You highlight to find things again later** → **Marqly.** Persistence plus semantic search over every highlight on Pro is the combination nobody else here has, and highlighting itself is free on every plan.
2. **Your highlights must end up in Notion, Obsidian or Anki automatically** → **Readwise Reader**, and pay the $119.88/year; the pipeline and the review loop are worth it for that workflow.
3. **You want your highlighting to be social, and you want the best export** → **Glasp**, understanding that free means public and private costs $150/year.
4. **You want free forever and want to own the files** → **Obsidian Web Clipper**, accepting that you configure sync and AI yourself.
5. **You want free forever and like annotating with other people** → **Hypothesis**, accepting one colour and a thin export path.
6. **You already keep your links in Raindrop** → **Raindrop Pro** at $28/year for unlimited highlights in four colours with notes.
7. **You are a student on a budget writing papers** → **Weava** with the 70% discount, exporting regularly, and knowing the extension has not been updated since February 2024.
8. **You want a citation-backed AI research assistant that also highlights** → **LINER**, if you accept that your highlights cannot leave.

The pattern across the category: capture is solved, retrieval is not, and portability is barely discussed. Any of these tools will paint text yellow. Two questions are worth paying for — whether you will ever see that highlight again, and whether you can take it with you if the answer changes.

## What actually matters in 2027

**Persistence and retrieval are different problems, and only one of them is solved.** Every tool here repaints or captures your marks. Almost none of them help you find a highlight six weeks later when you remember the idea and not the page. Semantic search over highlights exists in Marqly on Pro and in Karakeep if you self-host, and that is the whole list.

**Export is a feature you should shop for explicitly.** The spread runs from "already plain files on your disk" to "no export advertised," and the tools with the weakest exit paths are not the cheapest ones — LINER is one of the most expensive here. A highlight library you cannot leave with is a subscription you cannot cancel.

**Free is real in this category, but it is free with a condition.** Glasp's free highlights are public. LINER's free tier shows ads inside answers and grants no agent credits. Weava's free tier caps storage at 100MB, blocks sub-folders and has not had an extension update since February 2024. Obsidian's is free and asks you to configure your own sync and AI. Marqly's six-colour highlighting is free on every plan with highlight notes capped at 10, and its AI is Pro. Read the condition, not the checkbox.
